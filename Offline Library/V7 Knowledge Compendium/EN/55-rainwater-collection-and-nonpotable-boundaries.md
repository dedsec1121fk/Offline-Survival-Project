# Rainwater collection and non-potable boundaries

Rain falling from the sky does not automatically become safe drinking water after touching a roof, gutter, tarp or container. Collection surfaces can contribute dust, bird or rodent waste, metals, chemicals and debris. Emergency planning should distinguish water collection from water treatment and should label intended use clearly.

## Design the collection chain

Inspect the catchment, gutter or tarp before an emergency. Keep animal access, peeling coatings, fuel residues and unknown roofing materials out of the collection path. Use clean containers that can be closed and labelled. A dirty open bucket beside a road is not equivalent to a protected catchment simply because both contain rainwater.

## Separate uses

If water has not been verified or treated for drinking according to authoritative guidance, keep it physically separate from potable reserves. It may have a role in flushing, some cleaning or other non-potable tasks when local conditions allow, but the container must make that boundary obvious to every household member. Never rely on memory alone when several water types are stored together.

## After dry periods or contamination

The first runoff after dust, wildfire ash, bird activity or long dry weather may carry concentrated contamination. Industrial fallout or chemical-release conditions can make ordinary biological disinfection irrelevant; follow official do-not-use or water-quality notices instead of assuming boiling solves every hazard.

## Log each batch

Record date, catchment, weather context, container, visible condition, intended use and any treatment performed. Link it to the Water Batch module so nobody later mistakes an unverified batch for ready drinking water.

**Source anchors:** CDC/EPA emergency-water principles, WHO WASH guidance and local water authority rules. Rainwater regulations and safe uses vary by location.
