# Emergency communications: 112, concise messages, and fallback channels

## Emergency calling is for immediate danger
In Greece and throughout the European Union, 112 can be used free of charge when a person is in danger and needs immediate assistance. A useful emergency call or message starts with location, what has happened, how many people are affected, immediate hazards, and any critical access or medical constraint. Do not bury the location under a long story.

## Separate emergency traffic from family traffic
Calling emergency services and telling relatives you are safe are different tasks. Designate one out-of-area contact for household status so every person does not repeatedly call every other person. Prefer concise text messages when networks are congested and a voice conversation is not necessary, while recognizing that delivery can still be delayed or fail.

## Build channel redundancy
Record at least three channels appropriate to the household: mobile voice/text, an internet-based method when data works, radio where legally and practically available, and physical meeting points. A communications plan is not resilient if every method depends on the same phone, same power bank and same cellular network.

## Message format
Use a repeatable structure: `WHO – WHERE – STATUS – NEED – NEXT CHECK`. Example: “Loukas + 2, municipal shelter north entrance, all uninjured, need no assistance, next update 22:00.” For a distress message, replace the final elements with the specific urgent need and hazard. Avoid speculative casualty numbers or unverified road closures.

## 112 outbound alerts
Emergency authorities may also send location-relevant 112 alerts with protective instructions. Read the entire message, note the affected area and action, and follow the current instruction rather than forwarding a screenshot without context. If the household changes location, reassess which alert area now applies.
