# Offline navigation: map, compass, waypoints, and error checking

## A coordinate is not a route
Knowing latitude and longitude does not tell you whether a road, bridge, slope or shoreline is passable. Offline navigation should combine a paper/offline map, known landmarks, route notes, compass orientation and stored waypoints. Keep at least one method that does not depend on a phone battery.

## Calibrate your mental map
Before an emergency, mark home, work, school, hospital/clinic, assembly points, water sources, fuel, high ground, bridges, tunnels and alternative exits. Walk or drive important routes. Record where a route becomes unusable if one bridge closes or a coastal road is cut. A route you have never physically checked is only a hypothesis.

## Compass discipline
A compass helps maintain direction but can be influenced by nearby metal, vehicles, speakers, electrical equipment and magnets. Move away from obvious interference and compare the reading with terrain and known features. Phone compass sensors also require calibration and power. Do not follow a bearing blindly into a cliff, flood zone, private barrier or fire area.

## Waypoint quality
Store coordinates with a plain-language description: “north entrance of school assembly area,” not “WP7.” Include access notes, elevation if relevant, last verification date and why the point matters. If coordinates come from another person, verify the datum/format and plot them before relying on them.

## Position uncertainty
GPS accuracy can degrade near buildings, steep terrain, canopy or poor satellite geometry. If the marker jumps, stop and use multiple clues instead of walking farther to make the screen “agree.” In an evacuation, safe route status is more important than geometric shortest distance.

## Source anchors
- Official topographic/local maps and civil-protection evacuation maps.
- Device GNSS/compass manufacturer guidance.
- Offline Survival Project waypoint and route tools.
