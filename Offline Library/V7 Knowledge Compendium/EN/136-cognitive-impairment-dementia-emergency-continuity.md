# Cognitive Impairment and Dementia: Reducing Disorientation During Disruption

An emergency can remove the routines and landmarks that help a person with dementia or other cognitive impairment function safely. Noise, unfamiliar shelters, darkness, heat, rushed instructions, caregiver changes, and sleep loss can increase confusion or wandering risk. The plan should reduce decisions demanded from the person rather than expecting them to learn a complex emergency routine under stress.

## Preserve recognizable anchors
Keep a small set of familiar items, a simple written/visual daily routine, identification, medication list, preferred name, communication style, and calming activities with the evacuation kit. Use the same bag and the same place for essential objects whenever possible. In a new shelter, orient the person to toilet, sleeping place, caregiver, exits that staff permit residents to use, and the next planned activity.

## Design supervision as a shift task
Do not rely on “someone is watching.” Assign a named caregiver for each period and a named backup. Record handovers, last medication administration as maintained by the appropriate caregiver/clinical plan, food/fluid concerns, sleep, distress triggers, and any wandering attempt. If the person normally uses a tracking or identification device, plan what happens if its battery or network connection fails.

## Simplify communication
Give one instruction at a time, use familiar words, and show rather than repeatedly argue when possible. Avoid unnecessary relocation once a safe area is established. Sudden major behavior change, injury, fever, severe dehydration concern, or inability to provide necessary care warrants medical evaluation rather than being dismissed as “just confusion.”
