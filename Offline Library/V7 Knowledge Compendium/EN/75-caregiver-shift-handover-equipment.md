# Caregiver shift handover: transfer the condition, equipment and next decision—not just the name

## A handover should answer what changes next
For a dependent person, record current location, mobility/communication needs, food/water needs, medication schedule according to their established plan, equipment status, power reserve and the next time-sensitive task. Avoid a long biography that hides the immediate operating picture.

## Read back critical details
The incoming caregiver should repeat key times, equipment alarms/indicators they are expected to watch and escalation contacts. If a device has manufacturer instructions, keep them with the equipment. Do not change settings on medical equipment unless the person’s clinical plan authorizes it.

## Power continuity must be visible
Record which battery/source is active, estimated remaining reserve if known, which backup source is next and what condition triggers relocation or professional assistance. A label saying “battery charged” from yesterday is not enough during an ongoing outage.

## Track intake/output only when part of the person’s plan
Some care plans require clinical records; others do not. Use only the measurements the healthcare team has asked the household to track. Do not invent clinical monitoring merely because the project can store numbers.

## Protect rest for both caregiver and dependent person
A single exhausted caregiver is a continuity failure. Schedule relief, preserve sleep and identify who can safely perform each task. If no trained backup exists for a critical function, that gap should trigger earlier contact with healthcare/emergency support.

## Close uncertainty explicitly
Write “unknown” when something is unknown rather than carrying an assumption into the next shift. The handover is complete only when the receiving person knows the next action and who to call if the situation changes.
