# Gas odor, suspected leak and utility-isolation boundaries

A suspected fuel-gas leak is not a troubleshooting exercise. Odor, hissing, damaged piping, an appliance impact, earthquake damage or unexplained symptoms in an enclosed area should trigger distance, fresh air and professional response rather than experiments with switches or flames.

## Immediate priorities

Move people away from the suspected area and follow the gas provider, fire service and civil-protection instructions. Avoid actions that could create an ignition source in the affected space. Do not search for a leak with a match, lighter or improvised flame. If authorities tell you to evacuate, leave without delaying to complete a checklist.

## Shutoff knowledge belongs in preparedness

Learn in advance where the main shutoff is, what tool is required and whether residents are actually expected or permitted to operate it in your system. Label the location and keep access clear. A shutoff can create its own risks if a person does not understand the equipment; do not improvise on unfamiliar valves, damaged meters or pressurized systems.

## Re-entry and restoration

Treat a building with unresolved gas odor, damaged service equipment or fire damage as unavailable. Ventilation and service restoration should follow professional instructions. Do not reopen a supply because the smell seems weaker. After an earthquake, remember that electrical, water and structural problems may exist at the same time, so one cleared utility does not mean the building is fully safe.

## Household record

Keep provider contact details, meter/shutoff location, responsible adult, last inspection and any known appliance issues in the offline plan. During an event log time, odor/location, visible damage, evacuation, calls made and who gave clearance.

**Source anchors:** local gas utility, fire service and civil-protection guidance. This chapter intentionally avoids equipment-specific shutoff instructions because systems differ.
