# Life-threatening bleeding: first-aid boundaries for an offline reference

## Scene safety comes first
Severe bleeding may occur after debris, glass, vehicle crashes, tools, or structural damage. Do not become another casualty by entering moving traffic, unstable rubble, electrical hazards, fire, or contaminated water. Call the local emergency number early and use protective barriers such as gloves when available.

## Direct pressure is the core immediate action
American Red Cross guidance for life-threatening external bleeding starts with finding the source, placing a dressing where available, and applying firm, steady direct pressure. Keep pressure in place while emergency help is activated. Do not repeatedly lift the dressing to “check” the wound if that interrupts effective pressure. If blood soaks through, follow current first-aid training rather than removing clotting material casually.

## Tourniquets and wound packing are trained skills
Current first-aid systems may use tourniquets for severe limb bleeding and wound packing for certain deep wounds, but correct placement and use matter. The offline project should prompt trained responders to follow the technique they were taught and the device instructions; it should not pretend that reading one paragraph replaces hands-on training. Record the time a tourniquet was applied and communicate it to emergency medical personnel.

## Do not improvise invasive treatment
Do not probe a wound, push exposed tissue back into the body, remove a deeply embedded object, or apply chemicals, powders, coffee, ash, or household substances. If an object remains embedded, stabilise around it according to first-aid training and wait for qualified care. Keep the person protected from cold or overheating while monitoring responsiveness and breathing.

## Training and escalation
A household should practice first-aid activation, glove access, direct-pressure positioning, and emergency calling before a disaster. Severe bleeding, altered responsiveness, breathing difficulty, or signs of shock require emergency medical care. In Greece and the EU, use 112; where another local medical emergency number is designated, follow local instructions.
