# Possible Heart Attack: Chest Pain and Emergency Boundaries

A blackout, wildfire evacuation, heat event, or long drive can tempt people to explain chest symptoms as stress, dehydration, smoke, indigestion, or fatigue. A survival plan should instead make it easy to escalate possible heart-attack symptoms quickly and provide responders with the information they need.

## Warning patterns
CDC describes chest pain or discomfort—often pressure, squeezing, fullness, or pain—as a major warning sign, along with shortness of breath, weakness/light-headedness, cold sweating, and discomfort that may involve the jaw, neck, back, arms, or shoulders. Symptoms do not have to be dramatic, and not every person experiences the same pattern.

## Do not create a transport delay
Call emergency medical services when a heart attack is suspected. Do not make the person walk a long distance to a vehicle if ambulance access is possible, and do not delay to finish packing or secure property. If dispatch gives instructions, follow them. If the person becomes unresponsive and is not breathing normally, move into the CPR/AED response covered in the dedicated chapter.

## Medication boundaries
Do not invent an emergency medication plan from an offline guide. A person with known heart disease should keep their clinician-prescribed emergency plan and medicines accessible and use them exactly as instructed. Tell responders what was taken, when, and in what prescribed form. Avoid giving someone else’s prescription medicine.

## Outage-specific preparation
If the household relies on elevators, gated access, oxygen, powered medical equipment, or a remote location, plan how responders will reach the person. Keep address/location coordinates, floor/unit, gate instructions, and a brief medical card offline. During heat or smoke events, do not assume the environmental hazard is the only explanation for chest symptoms.
