# Offline Compass, Bearing, Declination, and Route-Card Fundamentals

A phone can provide coordinates and a compass, but a resilient navigation plan also works when the battery is low, the screen is broken or the downloaded map cannot load. Carry a physical compass and paper map when route failure would have serious consequences, and practice before the emergency.

## Understand the reference
A magnetic compass points toward magnetic north, while many maps are referenced to true or grid north. The difference varies by location and changes over time. Use the current declination information for the map/location and the method appropriate to your compass/map system; do not reuse an old adjustment from a different country.

## Use bearings as one layer, not blind steering
A bearing gives direction, not proof that the route is safe. Confirm terrain, roads, rivers, private/restricted land, wildfire/flood zones and daylight. Identify intermediate checkpoints so a small directional error does not grow over many kilometers.

## Build a paper route card
For each route write: start point, destination, major checkpoints, approximate distances, decision points, hazards, alternates, turnaround criteria, water points only if verified, and the person who knows the plan. Include coordinates in a consistent format and state the coordinate format/datum if relevant.

## Cross-check the phone
When the phone works, compare its compass to a known direction and move away from vehicles, speakers, magnets, steel structures and power equipment that can distort magnetic readings. Treat sudden heading jumps as a reason to verify, not as a reason to follow the screen faster.

## Lost procedure
Stop uncontrolled movement. Reconstruct the last known point, time and direction. Use map/terrain features and communicate location if possible. Do not turn a small navigation uncertainty into a major search area by continuing to walk without a plan.
