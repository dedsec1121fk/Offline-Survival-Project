# Lightning and thunderstorms: shelter decisions before the strike

## Thunder means the outdoor plan has failed
If thunder is audible, lightning is close enough to present a hazard. Move to a substantial enclosed building or an enclosed hard-topped vehicle rather than trying to identify a “safe” spot outside. Tents, open shelters, porches, isolated trees and small sheds do not provide reliable lightning protection.

## Inside is safer, not risk-free
During the storm, avoid direct contact with plumbing and unnecessary use of corded electrical equipment. Stay away from windows and doors when conditions are severe. Do not step back outside simply because rainfall weakens; lightning can occur away from the heaviest rain. The National Weather Service commonly advises remaining in safe shelter for at least 30 minutes after the last thunder.

## Outdoor activity planning
For hiking, beaches, sports, construction or field work, choose the shelter and trigger before the activity begins. The correct plan is not “we will run if lightning gets close,” but “when we hear thunder or receive the relevant warning, this group goes to this specific safe building/vehicle by this route.” A remote ridge, open field or shoreline may require turning around well before the storm arrives.

## If someone is struck
Lightning victims do not retain an electrical charge and can be touched. Activate emergency medical services and provide first aid/CPR according to your training and dispatcher instructions. Multiple casualties are possible; use scene awareness because continuing lightning can expose rescuers.

## Equipment and outage follow-on
A thunderstorm may also cause power loss, flash flooding, wind damage and falling branches. Once the immediate lightning threat passes, reassess roads, generators, damaged wiring and local flood warnings rather than assuming the incident is over.
