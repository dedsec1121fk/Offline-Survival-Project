# Smoke and carbon-monoxide alarms during outages: keep warning systems independent

## An outage can increase the need for alarms
Loss of mains power often leads households to use generators, fuel-burning heaters, fireplaces or alternative cooking equipment. Those choices can introduce carbon monoxide or fire risk. A power failure is therefore the wrong time to discover that every alarm depended on the same circuit or that backup batteries were never replaced.

## Inventory every warning device
Walk through the home and record smoke alarms, carbon-monoxide alarms and any combined units. Note location, power source, backup battery type, test date and replacement/end-of-life indication. Follow the device manufacturer’s instructions for testing and replacement; alarm lifetimes and signals are model-specific. Do not silence an alarm because the emergency is inconvenient—identify the cause and move to safety.

## Keep carbon-monoxide sources outside the living envelope
Portable generators and other fuel-burning engines must not be operated inside homes, garages or other enclosed/partly enclosed spaces. Exhaust can enter through openings. CDC emergency guidance emphasizes that carbon monoxide cannot be seen or smelled and can cause severe illness or death. Equipment placement must follow current manufacturer and local safety instructions, and an operating CO alarm is a backup warning—not permission to place an engine closer.

## Respond to an alarm, not to your guess
If a CO alarm activates or people develop concerning symptoms in a setting where combustion equipment is running, move to fresh air and contact emergency services/qualified guidance. Do not re-enter simply because the alarm stops outside. For a smoke alarm, investigate only when this can be done without entering smoke or delaying evacuation; fire escape comes before saving property.

## Outage inspection
After a long outage, check that alarms still show normal status and that batteries were not borrowed for another device. Restore any removed battery immediately. If a detector was exposed to water, heat, heavy smoke, impact or contamination, follow manufacturer replacement guidance rather than assuming a successful button test proves full function.

## Link this to the household power plan
Mark alarm batteries as protected stock. Keep the alarm type and replacement date in the maintenance register. During drills, make sure everyone can recognize the alarm sound and knows the outdoor meeting point. A household with backup electricity but no reliable warning system has not solved its outage risk.
