# Food allergy and special-diet continuity

## Emergency substitutions can create hidden risk
A disrupted supply chain often forces households to change brands, buy unfamiliar imported products, accept donated food, or eat in shelters. For a person with a serious food allergy, that is a safety problem rather than merely a preference issue. Keep a written list of allergens, tolerated staple foods, trusted brands where relevant, and cross-contact concerns. Do not assume two similarly named products have identical ingredients.

## Maintain an allergy-safe reserve separately
Store some clearly identified food that does not depend on the ordinary family pantry. Protect it from accidental consumption and from cross-contact with shared utensils. If a household member depends on a medically prescribed formula or specialised nutrition product, include supply-chain contacts and reorder lead time in the continuity plan rather than only recording calories.

## Donations and communal kitchens need a verification step
Unlabelled homemade food, food removed from its original package, or a communal dish with unknown utensils should be treated cautiously when the allergy is severe. Ask for the original label or ingredient record where possible. A volunteer’s assurance that a dish “should be fine” is not equivalent to verified ingredients and preparation.

## Emergency medication continuity belongs beside the food plan
People at risk of anaphylaxis should keep their prescribed emergency medication accessible and follow their clinician’s plan. The survival project should record where the medication is stored, expiry/refill timing, and who knows the emergency plan; it should not invent a substitute dose or tell someone to use another person’s prescription.

## Evacuation communication
Prepare a short bilingual card stating the allergy and the need to avoid cross-contact. In Greece, include a Greek version even if the household normally speaks another language. Show the card before accepting food rather than after symptoms appear.
