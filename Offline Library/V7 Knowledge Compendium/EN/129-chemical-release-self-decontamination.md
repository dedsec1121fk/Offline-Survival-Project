# Chemical Release Self-Decontamination: Get Away, Get It Off, Get Help

Chemical releases require a different mindset from ordinary dirt or smoke. The first objective is to leave the contaminated area or follow an official shelter instruction, then reduce the material remaining on the body without spreading it to other people, rooms, vehicles, or pets. CDC emergency guidance emphasizes rapid decontamination rather than waiting to identify the exact chemical before taking any action.

## Break the transfer chain
Move away from the release if officials say evacuation is safe, or remain in the instructed shelter zone if outside air is the greater hazard. Once in a safer place, keep potentially contaminated people and clothing away from clean sleeping, food, and medical areas. Avoid shaking clothing. Place removed items in a sealable bag or container and keep that package away from other occupants and animals until authorities give disposal instructions.

## Remove contamination gently
CDC advises removing clothing and washing exposed skin promptly after many chemical exposures. Blot visible liquid or material rather than aggressively rubbing it into skin. If clothing must pass over the head, avoid dragging contamination across the face when possible. Shower with water and soap when official guidance permits. Do not improvise neutralizing chemicals, solvents, bleach mixtures, or acids/alkalis on skin; an attempted “neutralization” can create heat, fumes, or a second injury.

## Escalate rather than experiment
Eye exposure, breathing difficulty, persistent coughing, burns, confusion, collapse, or a known hazardous-material release warrants urgent professional guidance. Do not induce vomiting after a swallowed chemical unless specifically instructed by poison-control or medical professionals. Preserve the product label, container name, official alert, and exposure time if this can be done safely; that information is more useful to clinicians than a home-made treatment guess.
