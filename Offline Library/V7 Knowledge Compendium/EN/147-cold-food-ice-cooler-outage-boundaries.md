# Refrigeration Failure: Ice, Coolers, Thermometers, and Food Decisions

A cooler can extend food safety during an outage only if it is treated as temperature-controlled storage rather than a box that contains ice. The household needs thermometers, a cold source, limited opening, separation from meltwater/contamination, and a clear discard decision when safe temperature history is lost.

## Protect temperature history
Record outage start and keep refrigerator/freezer doors closed as much as possible. CDC guidance notes that, when unopened, a refrigerator generally keeps food cold for about four hours and a full freezer about 48 hours (about 24 hours if half-full). These are planning figures, not permission to ignore actual food temperature or a longer/warmer exposure. Use an appliance or food thermometer where available.

## Build the cooler before the refrigerator warms
If an extended outage is expected and a safe ice/cold source is available, move priority perishables deliberately rather than repeatedly opening the refrigerator. Keep raw animal products contained so meltwater cannot contaminate ready-to-eat food. Replace melting ice as needed and monitor temperature. Do not taste questionable food to decide whether it is safe.

## Dry ice requires its own safety plan
Dry ice can keep items cold but produces carbon dioxide gas and can cause cold injury on contact. Use manufacturer/public-health safety guidance, appropriate gloves/tools, and ventilation; never create a sealed container that can pressurize. Do not place dry ice where children can handle it or in an occupied, poorly ventilated enclosure.

## Separate food decisions from medicine decisions
A cooler used for food is not automatically suitable for temperature-sensitive medicine, breast milk, or formula. Those materials have product/clinical storage rules of their own. Mark each container and preserve its temperature/time history separately.
