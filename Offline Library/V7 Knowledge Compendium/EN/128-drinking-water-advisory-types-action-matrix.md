# Drinking-Water Advisories: Distinguishing Boil, Do-Not-Drink, and Do-Not-Use

A household should not treat every water warning as a generic “boil it” problem. The action depends on the contaminant and on the exact wording issued by the water authority. CDC distinguishes boil-water, do-not-drink, and do-not-use advisories because boiling can control many biological hazards but cannot make every chemical or toxic contamination safe. A do-not-use notice is more restrictive still because skin contact or other use may also be unsafe.

## Convert the notice into household rules
Write the advisory type, affected address or zone, issue time, official source, and next review time at the top of the water board. Under it, list each activity separately: drinking, food preparation, ice, teeth brushing, infant feeding, dishwashing, handwashing, bathing, pet water, medication preparation, and cleaning. Use the authority’s instructions for each activity rather than assuming one rule applies to all of them. Mark containers that were filled before the advisory separately from water collected after the advisory began.

## Prevent a “safe-looking water” mistake
Clarity, smell, and taste cannot establish safety. Do not let a household filter create false confidence: during a boil-water advisory, CDC says filtered tap water may still need boiling. During a chemical do-not-drink warning, boiling may not remove the hazard and can be inappropriate. If the authority says do not use, isolate the tap source from routine household tasks until the restriction is lifted.

## Track the end of the advisory
Do not end restrictions because service pressure returns or the water looks normal. Record the official cancellation and any flushing, fixture, filter, ice-machine, or appliance instructions issued afterward. Replace or sanitize items only according to the applicable authority/manufacturer guidance. The command center can record the decision, but the local water authority remains the source of truth.
