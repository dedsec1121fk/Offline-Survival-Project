# Snakebite: First Response and Dangerous Myths to Avoid

Snakebite is a medical emergency when venomous species are possible. The most useful household response is calm, rapid medical access and prevention of additional tissue injury—not cutting, suction, ice, electric shock, or a tourniquet.

## Move away from the snake
Do not try to catch, kill, or handle the snake, including a dead or decapitated animal. Move the person and group out of striking range. If it can be done without approaching the animal, remember or photograph general appearance from a safe distance; identification should never delay care.

## Keep the person still and remove constriction
Call emergency medical services. Encourage the person to remain calm and limit unnecessary movement. Remove rings, watches, tight footwear, or other constricting items from the affected limb before swelling increases, if this can be done without manipulating the bite.

## Do not use destructive folk methods
CDC/NIOSH guidance specifically warns against tourniquets, cutting the wound, sucking venom, ice, immersion, electric shock, and folk therapies. These can worsen tissue damage or delay effective treatment. Do not wait for dramatic symptoms before seeking care.

## Record progression without causing delay
Note bite time, location, symptoms, and any change in swelling. If transport is delayed and a professional directs monitoring, mark/record the progression only in a way that does not constrict the limb. Do not give alcohol or use someone else’s medication as a substitute for antivenom/medical care.

## Greece and field planning
People working in scrub, farms, ruins, or remote trails should wear appropriate footwear, use a light at night, avoid placing hands into unseen holes/rock piles, and have an evacuation/communication plan. Keep pets away from snakes too.
