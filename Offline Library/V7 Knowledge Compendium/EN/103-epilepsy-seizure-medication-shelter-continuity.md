# Epilepsy and seizure continuity during evacuation, sheltering, and disrupted access

## Missed medication is an operational risk to plan around
For a person whose seizure control depends on regular medication, evacuation and pharmacy disruption can create risk even when the primary hazard does not cause injury. Keep the current medicine list, schedule, prescriber/pharmacy details, medical identification, and a protected carry supply according to clinician/pharmacy advice. Store it where it leaves with the person rather than in a distant household reserve.

## Teach the household the person’s established seizure plan
Caregivers, family, school/work contacts, and shelter companions who may be responsible should know the individual’s usual seizure pattern and what the treating clinician has instructed. General seizure first aid emphasizes protecting the person from injury, staying with them, and avoiding harmful myths such as restraining them or placing objects in the mouth. A personalized rescue-medication plan, if prescribed, must come from the clinician and product instructions.

## Build environmental protection into shelter selection
Falls, open flames, stairs, water, traffic, machinery, and extreme heat can make an otherwise manageable seizure more dangerous. Choose sleeping and waiting areas away from obvious secondary hazards. If the person has a seizure-related drowning risk, plan supervision and safer hygiene/water arrangements rather than assuming swimming ability removes that risk.

## Sleep, stress, illness, and schedule disruption matter
An evacuation can create sleep deprivation, missed meals, medication delays, alcohol exposure, illness, or flashing/visual triggers for some individuals. The plan should preserve the person’s known routine and clinician-identified triggers where practical. It should not claim that every seizure has the same trigger or that a household can prevent all seizures.

## Define when emergency help is required
Follow the person’s clinical seizure action plan and local emergency-service guidance. Households should know when a seizure is unusual for that person, prolonged beyond the action-plan threshold, repeated without recovery, associated with serious injury, breathing difficulty, water exposure, pregnancy, or another condition that requires urgent care. The offline manual does not replace emergency assessment.

## Make shelter staff aware without oversharing
If staying in a public shelter or evacuation center, tell appropriate staff what support is needed, how to contact the caregiver, and where essential medicine or rescue instructions are kept. Share the minimum health information necessary for safety.
