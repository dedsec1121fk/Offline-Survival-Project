# Cold Shelter: Condensation, Ventilation, and Moisture Control

A cold shelter can become wet from the inside even when the roof does not leak. Breathing, cooking, drying clothes and warm bodies add moisture; when that vapor reaches a cold surface it can condense into water that wets insulation and reduces comfort/safety.

## Keep wet and dry zones separate
Create an entry area for wet footwear and outer layers instead of bringing them directly into bedding. Store dry sleep clothing in a protected bag. If something must be dried, do it with ventilation and a safe heat source rather than sealing moisture into the sleeping area.

## Ventilate deliberately
More sealing is not always better. Maintain ventilation appropriate to the shelter and heating equipment. Never block required vents on fuel-burning appliances or use outdoor combustion devices indoors. If carbon-monoxide alarms are available, place/maintain them according to manufacturer guidance; an alarm does not make unsafe combustion acceptable.

## Protect insulation from compression and moisture
Insulation under the body matters because compressed sleeping bags provide less insulation beneath you. Use a dry ground pad/barrier. Shake/dry insulation when conditions permit and keep spare dry layers protected from the condensation zone.

## Manage people, not just temperature
Check for shivering, confusion, clumsiness, unusual sleepiness and wet clothing. Change wet clothing early. Do not wait for everyone to “feel cold enough” before adjusting layers or moving to a safer heated location.
