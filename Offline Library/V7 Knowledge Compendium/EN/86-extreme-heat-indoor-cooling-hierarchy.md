# Extreme heat indoors: use a cooling hierarchy before the building becomes unsafe

## Start with people at highest risk
Older adults, infants, pregnant people, outdoor workers and people with certain medical conditions or medicines can be more vulnerable to heat. Identify them before the hottest period and schedule check-ins. Follow clinician advice for individual conditions and medication storage.

## Reduce heat gain first
Shade sun-exposed windows where safe, close off hotter unused zones and reduce unnecessary heat-producing appliances. Ventilate when outside air is cooler/safer and close the building when outside conditions are hotter or smoky, according to the actual hazard. A wildfire-smoke day may require different ventilation choices than a clean-air heatwave.

## Use available electricity strategically
Fans can improve comfort in some conditions but are not a substitute for effective cooling during dangerous heat. If air conditioning is available on limited backup power, prioritize a small occupied zone and calculate the energy reserve. Do not run a generator indoors to power cooling.

## Protect drinking water and medication
Maintain safe fluids and easy access. Some medicines can affect heat tolerance or have storage requirements; CDC advises people to discuss heat/medication concerns with clinicians rather than changing medicines on their own. Move temperature-sensitive medicine according to professional/product guidance.

## Know the relocation trigger
If the indoor environment cannot be kept safe, use an official cooling center, trusted air-conditioned location or other safer shelter. Plan accessible transport and pet/service-animal needs before the peak. Relocation is a control measure, not a preparedness failure.

## Treat severe heat illness as urgent
Confusion, loss of consciousness or other signs of severe heat illness require emergency action. Follow current public-health/emergency guidance. The household planner should focus on prevention, early recognition and getting professional help.
