# Community Kitchen: Clean/Dirty Workflow and Allergen Control

Shared cooking can stretch fuel, labor and equipment, but it also concentrates food-safety and allergy risks. Treat a community kitchen as a small operation with roles and flow, not as many households improvising on one table.

## Draw the flow before cooking
Separate receiving, raw-food preparation, cooking, serving and dish/waste areas as far as the site allows. Keep raw animal products and their utensils away from ready-to-eat food. Assign handwashing access that does not require touching the potable-water dispensing spout with dirty hands.

## Control time and temperature
Use a food thermometer where relevant instead of judging doneness by color alone. Minimize the time perishable food remains in unsafe temperature conditions and follow current public-health guidance after refrigeration failure. If cold holding cannot be maintained, change the menu and batch size rather than pretending the cooler is a refrigerator.

## Make allergens visible
List major ingredients for each communal dish and keep the original package/label when possible. Use separate clean utensils/areas when an individual's allergy plan requires avoiding cross-contact. Do not promise that a meal is “allergen free” if the shared environment cannot support that claim.

## Assign sanitation responsibility
Someone must own surface cleaning, dish flow, waste removal and water-batch status. A single dirty cloth moved everywhere can spread contamination. Establish which cloth/brush belongs to food-contact cleaning and which belongs to floors/waste.

## Keep a simple meal log
Record date/time, dishes, source of high-risk ingredients, cold-storage status, cook/serve time and known allergy accommodations. If several people become ill, the log makes the exposure easier to reconstruct.
