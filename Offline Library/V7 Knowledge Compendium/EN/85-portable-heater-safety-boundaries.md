# Portable heating during an outage: warmth cannot come at the cost of fire or carbon monoxide

## Identify the heater type before deciding where it can operate
Electric heaters require a power source/circuit capable of the load. Fuel-burning heaters have ventilation, fuel and carbon-monoxide implications and some are not approved for indoor use. Follow the exact manufacturer and local fire-safety instructions; do not generalize rules from one heater to another.

## Keep combustion equipment out of sleeping improvisation
Never bring outdoor cooking or generator equipment indoors for heat. Carbon monoxide is invisible and odorless. A CO alarm is essential protection where combustion appliances are legitimately used, but it does not make prohibited indoor operation safe.

## Control clearance and stability
Keep portable heaters on stable surfaces with the manufacturer-required clearance from bedding, curtains, clothing, paper and furniture. Do not dry wet clothes directly over them. Route cords so they are not crushed, covered by rugs or placed across exits.

## Heat people and a zone, not the whole building
During limited power, a smaller occupied room may be easier to keep tolerable than the entire house, provided ventilation, fire separation and escape remain safe. Combine appropriate clothing and bedding so the heater does not carry the full load.

## Watch vulnerable occupants
Older adults, infants and people with health conditions may need a narrower safe temperature range. Have an early relocation trigger if the home cannot remain safe. Do not wait for severe cold symptoms before seeking a warmer location.

## Shutdown is part of use
Turn off/secure portable heat before leaving the room or sleeping unless the device is explicitly designed and approved for that unattended mode. Inspect after the outage for damaged cords, soot, unusual odor or fuel leaks before returning it to storage.
