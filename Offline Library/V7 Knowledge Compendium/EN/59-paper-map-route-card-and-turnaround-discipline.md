# Paper map, route card and turnaround discipline

Offline navigation becomes more resilient when a phone map is backed by a simple paper record that explains where the group intended to go. The paper does not need to be beautiful; it needs to remain readable after the battery is gone.

## Build a route card

Write start point, destination, primary route, alternate route, major junctions, water crossings, steep or exposed sections, planned rest points, vehicle location and a conservative turnaround time. Add the coordinates of only the few waypoints that matter; a page filled with numbers can be harder to use under stress than a short sequence of obvious checkpoints.

## Orient before moving

At every major junction compare the real terrain with map features and confirm the direction before walking or driving farther. If the map, compass, road sign and memory disagree, stop and resolve the disagreement while you still have a known point behind you. Do not use a compass bearing to justify crossing terrain that is visibly unsafe or officially closed.

## Protect the paper

Keep one route card with the traveller and another with the contact or vehicle when appropriate. Use pencil or waterproof storage if conditions demand it. Mark closures and changes with date/time rather than erasing the original route, so the handover explains why the group deviated.

## Turnaround is a safety tool

Define the time, daylight, weather, fuel/water reserve or physical condition that triggers return. A turnaround point prevents “almost there” thinking from consuming the reserve needed to get back.

**Source anchors:** official mapping/park guidance, local rescue advice and the project’s Navigation module. Map data can become stale; closures and emergency instructions override the route card.
