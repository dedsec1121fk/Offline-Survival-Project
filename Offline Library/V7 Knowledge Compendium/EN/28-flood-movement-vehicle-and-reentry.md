# Flood operations: movement, vehicles, contamination, and re-entry

## Water depth is not the only hazard
Floodwater can hide missing road surface, open drains, debris, electrical hazards, strong current, chemicals and sewage. A familiar street is not a known surface once covered. Never treat a barricade as an inconvenience to route around; responders may know of bridge, road or current hazards that are invisible from the vehicle.

## Evacuation before water reaches the route
If authorities advise evacuation, leaving before low roads flood is safer than waiting to see whether the water rises further. Identify high points and routes that do not depend on underpasses, stream crossings or a single bridge. Move essential medicines, mobility equipment, identity documents, chargers and animal supplies before the household is forced into a rushed departure.

## Vehicle decisions
Do not deliberately drive into floodwater. Depth, current and road integrity are difficult to judge from the driver’s seat, and a stalled vehicle can become a trap. If a planned route is flooded or barricaded, turn around and use an official alternative. Never stop on a bridge over fast-rising water simply to observe.

## After water enters a building
Assume floodwater and porous materials it touched may be contaminated. Avoid direct contact when possible, protect cuts, follow public-health cleanup guidance, and keep children and animals out of the affected zone. Electrical systems and appliances that were wet should not be energized until appropriately assessed.

## Re-entry is a staged process
Return only when local authorities permit it. Check access, structural movement, electrical/fuel hazards, drinking-water status, sewage function and mould/moisture separately. Photograph high-water marks and damage before cleanup when safe; track discarded items and expenses. If the building cannot be dried and made sanitary, continued occupancy may be a health problem even after the flood recedes.
