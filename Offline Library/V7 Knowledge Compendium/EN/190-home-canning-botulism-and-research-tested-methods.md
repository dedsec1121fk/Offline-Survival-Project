# Home Canning: Botulism Risk and Research-Tested Methods

Home-canned food can be a valuable shelf-stable reserve, but canning is not a place for improvising process time, pressure, jar size or recipe acidity. Low-acid foods can support botulism toxin production when processing is inadequate, and a contaminated jar may not look or smell spoiled.

## Use a tested recipe exactly
Use current recipes from a recognized research-based source such as the National Center for Home Food Preservation or USDA. Match food, preparation, jar size, canner type, processing time, pressure and altitude adjustments. Do not shorten a process because the food was “already hot,” the jar sealed, or a family recipe has “always worked.”

## Water-bath and pressure canning are not interchangeable
High-acid foods may use boiling-water canning when a tested recipe specifies it. Low-acid vegetables, meat, poultry and seafood generally require pressure canning under tested instructions. An electric multi-cooker is not automatically a validated pressure canner for a canning process.

## Treat suspicious jars as hazardous
Bulging, leaking, spurting, cracked or unsealed containers are warning signs, but absence of those signs does not prove safety. Never taste food to decide whether botulism toxin is present. If a product was canned by an unknown or unverified method, do not “rescue” it by tasting or casual reheating. Follow public-health disposal instructions.

## Keep a batch record
Record recipe/source, food, date, jar size, processing method/time/pressure and any altitude adjustment. A batch label makes later recalls or process mistakes traceable. Rotate stock and inspect seals before use.
