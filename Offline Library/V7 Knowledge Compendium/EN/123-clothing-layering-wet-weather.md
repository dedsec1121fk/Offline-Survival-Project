# Clothing in cold, rain and wind: manage moisture before adding more layers

## Start dry whenever possible
Wet clothing conducts heat away from the body and can make a mild temperature more dangerous, especially with wind. Keep at least one dry base layer and socks sealed inside the evacuation or shelter kit. Do not use that reserve for ordinary convenience; it exists for a person who becomes wet.

## Build layers with different jobs
A base layer manages moisture, an insulating layer traps warm air, and an outer layer protects from wind/rain. The exact garments depend on climate and activity. More insulation is not always better during hard physical work because sweat can soak inner layers and become a cold problem when activity stops.

## Vent before soaking
Open or remove an outer layer during exertion when conditions allow, then add insulation before resting. Protect head, hands and feet but avoid garments so tight that they impair circulation or movement. Replace wet gloves/socks early rather than waiting until they become a medical problem.

## Keep the clean/dry reserve separate
Use a waterproof inner bag or container rather than trusting the outside of a backpack. Floodwater-contaminated clothes do not belong in the same bag as dry sleep clothing. Mark one set as sleep/emergency reserve if the incident may last several days.

## Dry without creating a new hazard
Hang clothes where air can circulate but not directly against heaters, flames, generators or cooking equipment. Do not block exits. If indoor humidity is already high after flooding, drying large amounts of wet gear inside may worsen moisture problems; choose the driest safe area available.

## Recognize when clothing is no longer enough
Persistent shivering, confusion, loss of coordination, unusual drowsiness or skin changes from cold require prompt warming and medical evaluation according to current health guidance. Clothing management is prevention, not treatment for severe cold injury.
