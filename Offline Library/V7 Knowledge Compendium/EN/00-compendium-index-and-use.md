# V7 Knowledge Compendium: how to use this offline reference

This collection is designed as a decision-support library for household and small-group preparedness. It is not a replacement for emergency services, a clinician, a licensed engineer, an electrician, a utility operator, local law, or a current evacuation order. The material is intentionally organized around operational questions: what changes the decision, what can fail, what should be recorded, and when a problem exceeds household capability.

## Water, sanitation, and food
Chapters 01–10 cover reserve sizing, storage, boil-water advisories, disinfection boundaries, wells after flooding, limited-water hygiene, sewer failure, hand hygiene, waste and heat-related water planning. Chapters 11–14 continue into refrigerator/freezer triage, flood-exposed food, no-power meals and food-allergy continuity.

## Medical continuity and first-aid boundaries
Chapters 15–21 cover prescription continuity, electrically dependent medical equipment, severe bleeding, burns, choking, heat illness and cold exposure. These chapters emphasize recognition, immediate safety, professional escalation and continuity records; they deliberately avoid turning an offline project into a substitute for clinical diagnosis or individualized dosing instructions.

## Fire, earthquake, water, and weather hazards
Chapters 22–32 cover wildfire smoke, pre-evacuation, return after fire, earthquake actions, post-earthquake habitability, tsunami warnings, flood movement, lightning, severe wind, generator carbon monoxide and battery/load management. Each chapter separates the primary hazard from common secondary failures such as power loss, contaminated water, blocked routes or damaged utilities.

## Navigation, communications, people, and recovery
Chapters 33–40 cover offline navigation, vehicle evacuation, 112 communications, information verification, accessibility, children/older adults, animals/service animals, and recovery evidence. Use these alongside the Command Center registers so the plan contains names, routes, equipment and current status rather than generic advice.

## Greece-specific operations
Chapters 41–48 translate the general principles into Greek operational contexts: wildfire season, apartment earthquakes, urban heat with outages, island resupply interruption, 112 location reporting, coastal earthquake-to-tsunami transition, mountain snow/road isolation, and industrial or chemical releases. Current official instructions always override a stored manual.

## Extended household resilience and recovery
Chapters 49–64 deepen the edge cases that commonly break otherwise good plans: slope failure and rockfall, volcanic ash, home-fire escape, suspected gas leaks, sewage contamination, post-flood damp and mold, rainwater-use boundaries, small solar/USB charging, radio logging, lost-person self-rescue, paper route cards, fatigue and shift handover, psychological first aid, foodborne-illness clusters, biting-pest control, and structured community mutual aid. These chapters are deliberately operational rather than encyclopedic: each one explains what to observe, what to record, what not to improvise, and the point at which household action should give way to authorities or qualified professionals.

## Mobility, sheltering, utilities, and household continuity
Chapters 65–80 extend the operational chain into walking and bicycle evacuation, family reunification, neighborhood resource mapping, accountable mutual aid, supply intake, shared-shelter zoning, allergy control, animals in shelters, child routine/recovery, caregiver handover, accessible communications, waterproof documents, payment outages, damage photography, and after-action review. These are coordination chapters: they help a household or small group preserve accountability when several people and resources move at once.

## Infrastructure restoration and long-duration outage management
Chapters 81–97 cover repair readiness, utility shutoff maps, verification before restoring services, frozen-pipe/water damage, portable-heater boundaries, indoor heat management, cooling/warming-center routes, ash cleanup, post-flood drying, earthquake falling hazards, pre-disaster inventories, fuel logging, shared charging queues, official radio monitoring, offline information caches, timekeeping/shift schedules, and dependency single points of failure. The emphasis is on safe restoration and on knowing what must remain off, isolated, or professionally inspected.

## Specialist-care continuity and medically dependent households
Chapters 98–105 address specialist appointments, oxygen/CPAP continuity, pregnancy/postpartum needs, infant feeding when safe water or power is disrupted, diabetes/insulin continuity, epilepsy/seizure continuity, dialysis access, and chronic respiratory/cardiac risk during wildfire smoke. These chapters deliberately stop at continuity, recognition, records, and escalation; individualized treatment, dosing, and device settings remain under the person’s clinical team.

## Radiological emergencies and source verification
Chapter 106 separates radiation sheltering, contamination, food, water, medication, and evacuation decisions from ordinary microbial/chemical emergency rules. Chapter 107 is the maintenance register: it explains which public-health, environmental, civil-protection, weather, geological, clinical, and equipment sources should be rechecked before a future release changes safety-critical guidance.

## Household health, rationing, and outage injury prevention
Chapters 108–118 add infection-control/sick-room organization, older-adult isolation safeguards, grid-down cooking/fire/carbon-monoxide boundaries, water allocation, blackout movement and lighting, smoke/CO alarm continuity, temporary-cord/load limits, power-bank control, refrigerator/freezer temperature logging, use-first food sequencing, and water-container cleaning/rotation. These chapters focus on the quiet failures that produce injury or illness during a long outage even when the original hazard has passed.

## Hygiene, clothing, foot care, and high-rise living
Chapters 119–127 cover an emergency handwashing station, menstrual and incontinence continuity, separation of contaminated laundry, dry/warm bedding zones, clothing layers in wet weather, foot/blister prevention for evacuation mobility, high-rise utility loss, apartment stairwell/fire-smoke decisions, and water storage under apartment space/load limits. They are meant to turn basic supplies into maintainable routines during multi-day disruption.

## Source method
Safety-critical claims were checked against authoritative sources available during the v7 build, especially CDC, EPA, WHO, Ready.gov/FEMA, the US National Weather Service, the American Red Cross and the Greek Ministry for Climate Crisis and Civil Protection. The documents paraphrase guidance rather than reproducing source pages. Because official advice can change, every chapter names its source anchors so a future maintainer can re-verify it.

## Suggested offline workflow
1. Read the chapters that match local hazards before an emergency.
2. Turn the relevant guidance into household-specific data in the Command Center.
3. Run a drill and note where the plan relies on one person, one route, one device or one supply.
4. Print or export the few pages needed if the phone becomes unavailable.
5. During an incident, follow current authorities first and use the compendium to organize the details they do not manage for you.
