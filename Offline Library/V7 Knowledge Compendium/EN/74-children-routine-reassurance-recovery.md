# Children after disruption: use routine, honest information and participation without giving them adult burdens

## Predictability reduces uncertainty
After evacuation or a long outage, create a simple daily rhythm for waking, meals, hygiene, learning/play and sleep. The schedule does not need to copy normal life exactly; its value is that the child knows what happens next.

## Explain events at the child’s level
Use truthful, short explanations and correct rumors gently. Avoid exposing children continuously to alarming images, adult speculation or repeated disaster footage. If there is a 112/official instruction, explain the action being taken rather than debating frightening possibilities in front of them.

## Give small useful roles
A child may carry their own light, mark completed checklist items, help count sealed water bottles or choose a comfort item. The task should create participation, not make the child responsible for family safety or the welfare of younger siblings beyond what is age-appropriate.

## Keep reunification information simple
Children who are old enough should know their name, caregiver names, a phone number and the family meeting process. Put contact information in a secure card rather than expecting memory under stress. Follow school/shelter pickup rules.

## Watch behavior over time
Changes in sleep, appetite, concentration, play or fear can occur after frightening events. Offer reassurance and normal routines, and seek professional support when distress is severe, persistent or interferes with basic functioning. This manual does not diagnose mental-health conditions.

## Include children in recovery, not in hazards
They can help sort clean supplies, redraw the family map or talk through what made them feel safe. Keep them out of floodwater, ash, mold, sewage, unstable structures and hazardous cleanup. Recovery learning should not become exposure.
