# Floodwater, private wells, and household water isolation

## Treat flooding as a source-contamination event
Floodwater can carry sewage, fuel, agricultural chemicals, debris, and microorganisms. A well that has been submerged or surrounded by floodwater should not be assumed safe because the water looks clear. Isolate the well from normal household use and use a known safe alternative until the well can be assessed, disinfected where appropriate, and tested according to local health or water authority guidance.

## Protect people from the electrical and structural hazards first
Do not walk into a flooded pump room or basement to reach a well switch, pressure tank, or water heater if electrical equipment may be energised. Flood cleanup creates combined risks: contaminated water, unstable floors, sharp debris, mould, damaged wiring, and fuel or chemical containers. The water problem does not justify entering an unsafe space.

## Separate potable, cleaning, and discard streams
During recovery, label water by intended use. Known safe bottled or properly treated water should be protected for drinking and food use. Other water may be acceptable for limited cleaning only if the local authority says so. Water exposed to chemicals or sewage should not be “upgraded” by guesswork. Use the Water Batch system to keep source and status visible.

## Private-well recovery needs verification
A flooded well can need inspection of the casing, cap, electrical system, pump, and surrounding contamination pathways. Disinfection and testing are technical steps that vary with the well and local rules. Do not treat a single home chlorine procedure as proof of safety; testing after recovery is the decision point. If there is damage to the well head, casing, or electrical components, professional evaluation becomes more important.

## Household plumbing can retain contaminated water
Even after the external source is restored, building plumbing, softeners, filters, water heaters, ice makers, and connected appliances may contain old water. Follow the utility or health department’s flushing and cleaning instructions. Keep records of when the property switched back from emergency water so a later illness investigation or maintenance check has a clear timeline.
