# Dialysis continuity when roads, power, water, or treatment access fail

## Dialysis disruption can become time-critical
A disaster can close roads, interrupt electrical power or water, damage a dialysis center, displace the patient, or disrupt delivery of home-dialysis supplies. People receiving dialysis need an individualized emergency plan from their renal team before such conditions occur. The household role is to preserve contacts, transport, records, supplies, power continuity where applicable, and early communication—not to invent a substitute dialysis regimen.

## Build multiple contact and destination options
Record the usual dialysis center, renal clinician, supplier, transport provider, and at least one alternate treatment location or network contact if the care team provides one. Keep the dialysis prescription/summary and important medical information available for another facility. If a warning is issued days before likely disruption, contact the renal team early rather than waiting until a scheduled session is missed.

## Home dialysis needs infrastructure-specific planning
Home hemodialysis or peritoneal dialysis may depend on electricity, clean water, drainage, temperature-controlled supplies, sterile technique, lifting/storage space, and delivery services. Document which components are essential and what the manufacturer/renal program permits during outages. Never connect improvised water, generator, battery, or plumbing arrangements to medical equipment without explicit professional/equipment guidance.

## Diet and fluid changes require the renal team’s emergency instructions
CDC emergency information notes that dialysis access may be interrupted and points patients toward emergency renal guidance. Diet, fluid, potassium, sodium, and medication restrictions are individualized and can be dangerous if generalized. Keep the renal team’s written emergency diet/fluid instructions with the person; do not use a generic offline list as a replacement.

## Transport planning should activate early
If the patient cannot safely miss or delay treatment according to the renal team, road closure, fuel shortage, severe weather, wildfire, or evacuation orders should trigger relocation planning before the usual route is lost. Record mobility needs, wheelchair transfer requirements, oxygen/other equipment, and whether the destination can support them.

## After treatment resumes, reconcile what changed
Document missed/delayed sessions, substitute locations, medicine changes made by clinicians, symptoms, supply shortages, and transport failures. Replenish home supplies and update the plan while the failure chain is still clear.
