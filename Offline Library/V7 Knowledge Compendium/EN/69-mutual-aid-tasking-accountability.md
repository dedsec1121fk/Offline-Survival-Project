# Mutual aid tasking: help neighbors without losing accountability

## Good intentions need a task structure
During a disruption, several people may offer vehicles, tools, food or labor. Assign one clear task at a time: who is doing it, where, by when, what hazard is known and who they report back to. Avoid vague instructions such as “go see who needs help,” which can send volunteers into unsafe areas and produce duplicated effort.

## Do not exceed competence
Community members can carry supplies, share verified information and perform welfare checks, but should not take on hazardous electrical work, structural rescue, contaminated cleanup or medical procedures beyond training. Emergency services and qualified professionals remain responsible for specialized hazards.

## Track people in and out
For field tasks, record names, destination, departure time, expected return/check-in and communication method. If the route changes, update the coordinator. This is especially important when smoke, flood or communications outages make it difficult to know whether a delayed person is merely busy or in trouble.

## Match aid to the stated need
Ask before delivering. A household may need drinking water rather than food, accessible transport rather than a box of supplies, or a particular diet rather than generic meals. Unrequested donations can create storage and waste problems.

## Protect dignity and data
Do not publicize who received medication, financial help or disability support. Share enough information to coordinate the task, not a person’s full private history. Photographs of recipients should not be a condition of assistance.

## Close every task
The person reports outcome, unmet need, resources consumed and any new hazard. Update the decision board instead of leaving a verbal promise floating between shifts. Mutual aid becomes dependable when tasks are closed and learned from.
