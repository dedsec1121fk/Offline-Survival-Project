# Hearing and Vision Assistive Technology During an Outage

Assistive technology failures can turn a manageable outage into an information and mobility emergency. Hearing aids, cochlear-implant processors, magnifiers, electronic braille devices, screen readers, low-vision lighting, and phone accessibility features may depend on batteries, charging, internet access, or a specific caregiver workflow.

## Inventory the whole communication chain
List the device, charger/cable, spare batteries, cleaning/drying supplies, protective case, compatible backup device, and any adapter required for portable power. Store written or printed communication cards for situations where speech, hearing, vision, or phone access is impaired. Critical instructions—medicines, evacuation assistance, allergies, contact person, and meeting point—should not exist only inside one powered device.

## Build redundancy around the person
Agree on at least two ways to receive alerts: for example visual plus vibration, spoken plus written, or a buddy system plus official phone alert. Mark who checks on the person if the usual alert method fails. Keep pathways and essential objects in consistent positions; moving furniture or supplies during a dark outage can create a new fall/collision hazard for someone relying on memorized layout.

## Protect batteries without exhausting them
Do not keep every device charging continuously from a limited reserve. Prioritize the assistive device needed for immediate safety and communication, schedule recharge windows, and preserve a manual fallback where possible. If a rechargeable hearing/vision device becomes unavailable, shift early to the agreed communication method rather than waiting for complete power loss.
