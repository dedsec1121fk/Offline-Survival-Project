# High-Rise Elevator Loss: Mobility and Evacuation Planning

Elevator loss changes a high-rise emergency plan long before a fire occurs. A person who can normally leave independently may become dependent on stairs, a transfer device, a trained helper, or a protected waiting/assistance procedure defined by the building and local fire service. The plan therefore begins with building-specific information, not an improvised stair carry.

## Know the building’s real options
Identify at least two stair routes where the building provides them, areas of refuge or protected waiting spaces if officially designated, fire-alarm locations, emergency intercoms, and who manages evacuation assistance. Ready.gov advises using stairs rather than elevators in emergency evacuation contexts unless responders direct otherwise. Ask the building manager how mobility-limited residents are included in the official plan and whether evacuation chairs exist, who may use them, and what training is required.

## Separate power outage from fire
During a simple outage, remaining in a safe apartment may be preferable to an unsafe manual descent; during fire, smoke, structural danger, or an official evacuation order, the decision can be entirely different. Do not treat an elevator returning to service as proof that the building is safe. Keep medication, water, communication devices, mobility aids, shoes, and identification accessible so a delayed evacuation does not become a rushed packing event.

## Never improvise a dangerous carry
Untrained people carrying a wheelchair or person down stairs can injure both the occupant and helpers. Pre-arrange assistance with the building, care network, or emergency services, and practice only the procedures and equipment that responsible organizations approve.
