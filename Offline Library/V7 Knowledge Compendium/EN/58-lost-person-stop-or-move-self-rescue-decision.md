# Lost-person self-rescue: stop or move decision

Becoming disoriented is often made worse by continued movement without a verified destination. The objective is not to prove navigation skill; it is to reduce exposure, make your position easier to find and avoid converting a manageable error into injury or separation.

## Recognize the loss of certainty

Stop when landmarks no longer match the map, the route has disappeared, daylight is becoming limited, weather is worsening, a person is exhausted or repeated “shortcuts” fail to reconnect with a known point. Record the last place you were certain of, approximate time, direction travelled and any distinctive feature passed since then.

## Decide whether movement improves safety

Moving may be appropriate when there is an immediate hazard such as fire, rising water, rockfall or dangerous cold exposure and a clearly safer location is known. Otherwise, random searching can make a rescue area larger. Follow the local rescue authority’s guidance and the plan left with your contact. Do not descend cliffs, enter water, caves or closed terrain merely because they appear to shorten the route.

## Make yourself findable

Use visible clothing, light or signalling equipment appropriate to the environment without creating a fire or electrical hazard. Conserve phone battery after communicating useful coordinates, route name, group status and urgent medical needs. If coverage is intermittent, use scheduled checks rather than constant searching when that is safe.

## Household prevention

Leave route, destination, turnaround time, vehicle location and overdue action with a reliable person before departure. Carry a paper map or route note even when using a phone.

**Source anchors:** local mountain/rescue service instructions, civil protection and park/trail rules. This chapter does not prescribe a universal survival signal because approved methods vary by country and environment.
