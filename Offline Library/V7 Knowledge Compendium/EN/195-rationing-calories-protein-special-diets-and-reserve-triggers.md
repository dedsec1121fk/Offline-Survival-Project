# Rationing Food: Calories, Protein, Special Diets, and Reserve Triggers

Emergency rationing should reduce waste and match activity before it becomes arbitrary deprivation. The goal is to know how long food can support the group, which foods are medically important, and when the situation requires resupply or relocation.

## Start from the people, not just calories
Record household size, ages, pregnancy/lactation, allergies, diabetes or other diet-sensitive conditions, texture/swallowing needs and typical activity. A single “calories per person” number cannot represent all of those needs. Use clinical diet plans where they exist and do not override them with a generic survival table.

## Count usable food
Inventory food by portions/energy only if labels or reliable recipes make that meaningful. Subtract spoiled, contaminated or inaccessible food. Separate items that require large amounts of cooking water/fuel from ready-to-eat foods. Track protein and diet-specific staples, not only total calories.

## Create decision thresholds
Define a minimum reserve that is not consumed under normal shelter-in-place operations. When forecasted coverage reaches the trigger, act: reduce waste, simplify menus, seek verified distribution, coordinate with neighbors or prepare relocation. Waiting until the final day's food removes choices.

## Adjust work to the reserve
Heavy unnecessary labor increases energy and water demand. During scarcity, organize tasks, avoid duplicated trips and shift strenuous work to safer temperatures. Rationing is partly demand management.

## Avoid unsafe “stretching”
Do not dilute infant formula, consume spoiled food, eat unknown plants or use foods that violate a person's serious allergy/medical restrictions simply to increase days on paper.
