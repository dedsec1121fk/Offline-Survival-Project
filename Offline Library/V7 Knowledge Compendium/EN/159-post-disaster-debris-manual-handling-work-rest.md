# Debris Cleanup: Manual Handling, Team Lifts, and Work-Rest Control

Post-disaster cleanup injuries often come from ordinary actions repeated under abnormal conditions: lifting wet material, twisting with debris, carrying bulky objects through poor footing, working in heat, and continuing after fatigue. The safest plan limits exposure before pain or exhaustion forces a stop.

## Survey the load and route first
Before lifting, identify sharp edges, contamination, unstable piles, overhead hazards, stairs, slippery surfaces, and where the material will be placed. Use mechanical assistance when available. CDC/NIOSH disaster guidance recommends team handling for bulky objects and warns against excessive single-person lifting.

## Rotate tasks instead of only people
Moving the same type of load for hours can overload the same muscles even when workers switch occasionally. Alternate lifting, sorting, documentation, rest, hydration, and lighter tasks. Stop work for heat illness symptoms, loss of coordination, worsening pain, unsafe light, structural movement, or changing weather.

## Keep contaminated debris out of living routes
Create a disposal path that does not cross food preparation, clean water, sleeping, or medical zones. Bag or contain sharp/small contaminated items appropriately. Do not let a faster cleanup route create a second contamination problem inside the shelter.

## Record injuries and near-misses
A dropped object, almost-fall, torn glove, or unexpected pile movement is useful information. Log it and change the route/tool/team arrangement before the next load.
