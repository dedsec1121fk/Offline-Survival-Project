# Infant Diapering and Hygiene When Water Is Limited

Infant care can consume clean water quickly because feeding, hand hygiene, diapering, bottle/equipment cleaning, and laundry all compete for the same reserve. The household should protect the clean-water chain around feeding and hand hygiene rather than using drinking-quality water indiscriminately for every cleanup task.

## Create a one-way diaper station
Separate clean diapers, wipes, barrier products, changing surface covers, waste bags, and clean clothing from used materials. Position the station away from food preparation and infant feeding equipment. Keep a dedicated waste container and remove waste on a schedule before it attracts insects or contaminates the living area. After diaper changes, prioritize caregiver hand hygiene using the safest method available under the current water advisory.

## Do not let shortage blur water-status labels
If tap water is under an advisory, follow the authority’s instruction for handwashing, bathing, and cleaning; boil-water, do-not-drink, and do-not-use notices are not interchangeable. Keep water intended for formula preparation or drinking in clearly marked containers that are not dipped into with dirty cups or hands. Ready-to-feed formula can reduce dependence on safe mixing water when formula feeding is necessary during an emergency, according to CDC emergency infant-feeding guidance.

## Watch the infant, not only the supply count
A diaper log can help caregivers notice a meaningful change in feeding or wet diapers, but the app should not diagnose dehydration. Repeated vomiting, unusual sleepiness, poor feeding, markedly fewer wet diapers, fever, or an infant who appears unwell should prompt timely clinical advice. Record concerns for the next caregiver and avoid waiting for a shift change if symptoms are urgent.
