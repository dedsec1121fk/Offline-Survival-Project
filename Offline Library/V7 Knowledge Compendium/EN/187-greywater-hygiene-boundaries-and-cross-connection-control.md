# Greywater: Hygiene Boundaries and Cross-Connection Control

Greywater can reduce demand on potable water, but it is not clean water. Water from bathing, handwashing or laundry can contain microorganisms, detergents, skin material and chemicals. Kitchen and toilet waste may be classified differently and should not be casually mixed into a greywater plan.

## Keep potable and reuse systems unmistakably separate
Use labels, dedicated containers and physically separate hoses/lines. Do not dip a drinking-water scoop into a greywater container. Prevent backflow or cross-connection into potable plumbing. If household members cannot reliably distinguish two systems under stress or darkness, simplify the setup.

## Match reuse to exposure risk
Choose uses that minimize human contact and aerosol generation, following local rules. Avoid storing untreated greywater for long periods; microbial growth and odor can increase quickly. Do not use greywater where it can contact food that will be eaten raw unless authoritative local guidance specifically supports that use and treatment.

## Track chemicals
Bleach, solvents, strong cleaners, medication residues and high-salt detergents can change whether a batch is suitable for reuse. A single contaminated laundry load can make the whole collection unsuitable for the planned destination. When in doubt, route it to the approved waste path rather than trying to “fix” it with another chemical.

## Outage planning
If sewer service is impaired, do not increase greywater discharge into a system that is already backing up. Sanitation and wastewater authority instructions override your normal reuse plan.
