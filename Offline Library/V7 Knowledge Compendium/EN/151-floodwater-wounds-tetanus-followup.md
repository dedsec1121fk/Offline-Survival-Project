# Floodwater Wounds: Cleaning, Documentation, and Tetanus Follow-Up

Floodwater can carry sewage, debris, chemicals, and sharp objects. A cut acquired during cleanup should not disappear into the incident log as a minor inconvenience, because contamination and vaccination history may affect follow-up.

## Get the wound out of floodwater
Stop work, move to a clean area, and clean open wounds with soap and clean water as CDC disaster guidance recommends. Cover the wound appropriately for protection and prevent further floodwater contact. Deep punctures, bites, severe contamination, significant bleeding, loss of function, or signs of infection require professional assessment rather than continued cleanup.

## Preserve the facts clinicians need
Record time, object/mechanism, whether floodwater or sewage contacted the wound, initial cleaning, current symptoms, and the person’s known tetanus vaccination history if available. Do not guess the date; mark it unknown when uncertain. Healthcare professionals can determine whether vaccination or other treatment is needed based on wound type and immunization history.

## Fix the work process too
A cleanup injury should trigger a review of gloves/boots, lighting, debris handling, tool choice, fatigue, and staffing. If one person was cut because sharp debris was carried loose through a dark route, treating only the wound leaves the next worker exposed to the same hazard.
