# Flood drying and moisture triage: remove water, decide what can dry, and stop hidden dampness

## Re-entry safety comes first
Do not start drying a building until structural, electrical, gas/fuel and contamination hazards are addressed. Floodwater can contain sewage and chemicals. Use current cleanup guidance and professional help for major contamination, structural damage or extensive mold.

## Stop the source
Drying cannot succeed while rain, pipe leakage, groundwater or roof damage continues. Identify and control the incoming water when safe. Separate clean rainwater damage from sewage/flood contamination because salvage decisions differ.

## Prioritize time-sensitive materials
Standing water and wet porous materials can support microbial growth. Remove water safely, increase drying/ventilation when outdoor air conditions permit, and expose hidden wet areas where this can be done without damaging structural/safety systems. Large soaked insulation, wall assemblies and flooring may need professional assessment.

## Measure progress instead of judging the surface
A wall can feel dry outside while remaining wet internally. Use moisture measurements if available and compare to known-dry areas/material guidance. Record rooms and dates. Do not close walls or reinstall finishes merely because visible water disappeared.

## Avoid contaminating the clean zone
Tools, footwear and clothing from flood cleanup remain on the dirty side until cleaned. Protect unaffected rooms from dust and demolition debris. Keep food preparation and sleeping away from active contaminated cleanup.

## Know when the job exceeds household scale
Extensive sewage exposure, structural damage, large mold growth, hazardous materials or inability to dry the building promptly justify professional remediation/public-health guidance. The objective is a dry, safe structure—not merely a room that looks cleaner.
