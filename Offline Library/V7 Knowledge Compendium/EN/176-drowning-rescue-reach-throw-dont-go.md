# Drowning Rescue: Reach, Throw, and Avoid the Second Victim

A person in distress in water can unintentionally pull an untrained rescuer under. Floodwater adds current, hidden drains, contamination, electricity, and debris. The first rule is therefore scene safety and a rescue method that does not turn one victim into two.

## Raise the alarm early
Call emergency services/lifeguards as soon as a person is in serious difficulty. Give a fixed landmark, coordinates if available, current direction, clothing, flotation status, and the last point where the person was seen. Assign one person to keep visual contact rather than having everyone run for equipment.

## Prefer rescue from outside the water
Use a pole, branch, paddle, rope, throw bag, life ring, flotation device, or boat when these can be used safely. Keep your own body low/anchored when reaching from an edge so the person cannot easily pull you in. Do not enter fast floodwater, surf, cold water, or an unknown depth unless you are trained/equipped for that rescue environment.

## After removal
Check responsiveness and breathing. If the person is not breathing normally, begin CPR and use an AED according to training/dispatch guidance. If they are breathing but impaired, protect the airway, keep them warm, and obtain medical evaluation. Drowning complications can persist after apparent initial recovery.

## Floodwater is not a swimming pool
Do not assume a street is safe because the water looks shallow. Open manholes, storm drains, current around vehicles, contaminated water, and energized utilities can make entry lethal. Professional flood rescue may require ropes, boats, flotation and protective equipment unavailable to a household.

## Prepare the rescue environment
At boats, pools, wells, cisterns, docks, or flood-prone properties, pre-position throw/reach equipment and teach household members how to use it. Prevention and non-entry rescue are more reliable than relying on a strong swimmer.
