# After-action review: convert the incident into specific changes instead of a story

## Review soon enough to remember details
Hold a short review after immediate safety and recovery pressures have stabilized. Use logs, check-ins, photos and actual resource records rather than relying only on memory. The goal is not to assign blame; it is to find which assumptions failed and which actions worked.

## Reconstruct a timeline
Mark the first warning, key decisions, departures, power/water loss, communications failures, resource thresholds and restoration. Differences between planned and actual timing often reveal the most important weakness—such as a route that took twice as long or a battery reserve that lasted half the estimate.

## Separate observations from judgments
Write “the freezer reached X at Y time” or “the alternate road was closed” before writing “we were unprepared.” Specific observations can produce corrective actions; broad judgments usually cannot.

## Identify one owner and deadline for each correction
Examples: replace a failed cable, move documents to a waterproof pouch, add a second contact, practice the stair route, change the evacuation trigger. A lesson without an owner/date becomes a note nobody acts on.

## Remove unnecessary complexity
Not every problem requires buying equipment. If a checklist was too long, simplify it. If two logs duplicated information, combine them. If a gadget was never used, decide whether it belongs in the kit. Improvement includes subtraction.

## Test the correction
After changes, run a focused drill that reproduces the failed condition safely. Close the improvement only when the new process works. Keep the before/after note in the training history so future household members understand why the rule exists.
