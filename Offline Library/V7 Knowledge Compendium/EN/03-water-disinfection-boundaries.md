# Emergency water disinfection: methods and safety boundaries

## Start by identifying the hazard
Emergency disinfection is aimed primarily at disease-causing microorganisms. It does not reliably remove fuel, solvents, pesticides, salt, many metals, or unknown industrial contamination. If authorities identify a chemical or radiological hazard, use the water source and treatment method they specify. The safest household decision can be to abandon a source rather than applying more treatment to water whose contamination is unknown.

## Boiling is the preferred germ-control method when feasible
CDC identifies boiling as the best household method for killing germs in emergency water. Clear water should reach a rolling boil for one minute, or three minutes at high elevation under CDC guidance. Fuel, ventilation, burn risk, and the ability to cool and store the treated water are part of the method; a treatment step that causes a fire, carbon-monoxide exposure, or scald injury is not a successful emergency process.

## Chemical disinfection requires the current label and concentration
EPA and CDC provide bleach-based emergency methods, but the correct amount depends on the sodium-hypochlorite concentration and the water condition. Only plain, unscented household chlorine bleach that is suitable for disinfection should be considered; scented, colour-safe, splashless, or cleaner-added products may not be appropriate. Because product concentrations change, keep the actual bottle and current official instructions together instead of memorising one universal number.

## Cloudy water needs pretreatment
Visible particles protect microorganisms and make measurement harder. Let sediment settle or pass water through a clean cloth, paper towel, or coffee filter before the verified disinfection step. This pretreatment improves clarity but is not itself disinfection. Keep “filtered” and “treated” as separate states in your log so a person on the next shift does not drink water that was only clarified.

## Containers can re-contaminate treated water
Clean and sanitize storage containers before filling them, avoid dipping hands or cups into the reserve, and use a narrow opening or tap when possible. If a cap falls onto a dirty surface or floodwater reaches the outside and opening of the container, isolate it until it can be cleaned or replaced. The treatment history is only useful if the water remains protected afterward.
