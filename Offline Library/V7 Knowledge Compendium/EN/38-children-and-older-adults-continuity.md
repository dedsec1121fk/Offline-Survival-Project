# Children and older adults: continuity when routines disappear

## Routine is a safety tool
Disasters disrupt sleep, meals, medication timing, orientation, school, caregivers and familiar places. For children and older adults, preserving a few predictable anchors—who is responsible, where the next meal comes from, when medicine is due, where the toilet is, when the next update happens—can reduce confusion and missed care.

## Child-specific preparation
Keep age-appropriate identification, caregiver contacts, medications, feeding supplies, hygiene items, comfort object and any required restraint/car seat ready for evacuation. Make reunification plans explicit: which adults are authorized to collect the child and what happens if the primary school/day-care route is closed. Teach simple actions appropriate to the child’s age without making the child responsible for adult emergency decisions.

## Older-adult continuity
Record hearing/vision aids, mobility devices, medication schedule, cognitive/orientation needs, dietary restrictions and the names of people who can assist. Keep spare batteries or charging methods for essential aids. If a person is at risk of wandering or becoming disoriented, the plan should include supervision and identification rather than relying on verbal instructions alone.

## Heat, cold, and dehydration
Vulnerability can increase during temperature extremes and water interruption. Assign an adult to conduct direct checks at defined intervals. “They have not called us” is not a check. Watch for changes from the person’s normal behaviour, intake, mobility or alertness and seek medical guidance when concerning changes appear.

## Psychological aftermath
Give accurate, age-appropriate information and avoid repeatedly exposing children or cognitively vulnerable adults to distressing rumor streams. Restore ordinary routines as conditions allow. Persistent severe distress, functional decline or inability to provide required care is a reason to seek professional support.
