# Carbon Monoxide Incident Response During an Outage

Carbon monoxide is especially dangerous during outages because the substitute equipment people introduce—generators, grills, camp stoves, engines, and improvised heaters—can create an invisible, odorless poison. A CO alarm or unexplained illness affecting several occupants should be treated as an environment problem first, not as a puzzle to solve indoors.

## Leave the exposure zone
If a CO alarm sounds or occupants develop concerning symptoms around fuel-burning equipment, move people and animals to fresh air and contact emergency services or the appropriate medical service. Do not stay inside to search room by room for the source. A person who becomes weak or confused may not be able to evacuate independently, so the household plan should identify who assists children, older adults, and mobility-limited members.

## Do not “ventilate and retry” a dangerous setup
Opening a garage door or window does not make indoor generator use safe. CDC guidance places portable generators outdoors and well away from doors, windows, and vents, and recommends working CO alarms with battery capability. Never use charcoal grills, camping stoves, or fuel-burning engines inside living areas, tents, garages, or other enclosed/partly enclosed spaces. Equipment that repeatedly triggers alarms should remain out of service until the hazard is professionally resolved.

## Record the incident for the next shift
Log what equipment was operating, where it was located, alarm times, who was exposed, whether anyone needed medical assessment, and what corrective action was taken. This prevents a later shift from reconnecting the same hazardous device because “the power is still out.” Replace depleted detector batteries and preserve the detector model/manual for maintenance checks.
