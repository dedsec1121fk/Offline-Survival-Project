# Accessibility continuity: evacuation that works for the actual person

## Build around functional needs, not labels
Two people with the same diagnosis can require very different emergency support. Record the specific function that must continue: walking, transferring, seeing, hearing, communicating, understanding instructions, regulating sensory input, powering equipment, refrigerating medicine, using the toilet, eating a particular texture, or being accompanied by a support person.

## Duplicate the critical pathway
If one caregiver, lift, charger, wheelchair battery, hearing-aid supply or communication device is the only way a person can evacuate, the plan has a single point of failure. Identify a backup person and, where possible, a backup method. Store model/serial information and concise operating notes for equipment that another helper may need to use.

## Make alerts accessible
A siren alone may not work for someone who is deaf; a text alert alone may not work for someone who cannot see or read it; a complex message may not work for someone under cognitive stress. Pair channels: sound, vibration, visual cue and direct human check. During a real incident, confirm understanding rather than assuming message delivery equals comprehension.

## Transport and shelter compatibility
Pre-identify whether the evacuation vehicle can load the mobility device and whether the destination has the required access, power, toilet, refrigeration, quiet space or support. “Shelter available” is not enough if the person cannot enter or safely remain there. Carry a compact personal support card that states communication method, assistance needed, essential equipment and emergency contact.

## Protect autonomy
Ask the person how they want assistance provided and include them in drills. Do not separate a person from mobility, communication or service-animal support without a safety reason. During handover, describe functional needs accurately rather than reducing the person to a diagnosis.
