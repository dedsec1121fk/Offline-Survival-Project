# Hygiene, Infection Control and a Temporary Sick Area

## Reduce transmission before diagnosing
During evacuation, outage or crowded sheltering, vomiting, diarrhea, cough and fever can spread quickly even when the cause is unknown. Focus first on clean hands, safe food/water, cleaning high-contact surfaces, practical separation of obviously ill people and timely medical evaluation for danger signs.

## Put hand cleaning where it matters
A handwashing station beside the temporary toilet, food area or caregiving space is more useful than one far away. Provide soap, safe water and drainage that does not contaminate feet or supplies. Sanitizer can be a temporary alternative in suitable situations but visibly dirty hands still need proper washing.

## Give the ill person a defined zone
When practical, assign one rest/sleep area, dedicated drinking vessel, waste container and regularly cleaned high-touch surfaces. Ventilate when appropriate for the incident; during smoke or chemical sheltering, opening windows may conflict with air-protection instructions.

## Limit close contacts
A single primary caregiver can reduce unnecessary exposure and improve consistency of fluids, medicines and symptom logging. The caregiver needs clear hand-hygiene and waste routines plus criteria for escalating to professional care.

## Protect food operations
Someone with vomiting or diarrhea should not prepare communal food when another person can take over. Keep raw and ready-to-eat foods separate, use safe water and do not share cups, utensils, towels or toothbrushes.

## Protect wounds
Keep clean open wounds covered and away from floodwater or sewage. After exposure, wash appropriately with safe water and soap. Increasing redness, swelling, drainage, severe pain, fever or rapid deterioration needs medical evaluation.

## Keep chemical cleaning within label boundaries
Follow disinfectant labels, ventilate when the incident permits and never mix products such as bleach with ammonia or acids. Surface disinfectants are not skin treatments.

## Log symptoms without creating a diagnosis engine
Record onset time, measured temperature if available, vomiting/diarrhea, breathing difficulty, intake concerns, rash, exposure history and medicines already taken. A timeline supports clinicians and handovers; the software should not convert it into self-diagnosis or dosing advice.

## Escalate emergency signs
Severe breathing difficulty, altered consciousness, shock, serious bleeding, worsening dehydration or rapidly spreading infection require professional help. Use local emergency services and clinical advice rather than extending household isolation beyond its safe limits.
