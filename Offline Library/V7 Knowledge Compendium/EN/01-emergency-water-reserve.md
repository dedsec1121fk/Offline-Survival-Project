# Emergency water reserve: quantity, rotation, and protected storage

## Planning the reserve
An emergency water reserve should be treated as several separate capabilities: water for drinking, food preparation, oral hygiene, basic washing, pets, and any medical or accessibility need that increases consumption. CDC household guidance uses at least one US gallon (about 3.8 litres) per person per day for three days as a minimum planning baseline and encourages a two-week supply when practical. Hot climates, illness, pregnancy, nursing, heavy work, pets, and interruptions to normal sanitation can push real use above a simple baseline, so the household plan should record both a minimum survival reserve and a larger target.

## Containers and storage location
Use food-grade containers intended for water. Protect them from fuel, pesticides, cleaning chemicals, direct sun, floodwater, and places where a damaged shelf could crush or puncture them. A single large container is efficient but creates one failure point; several manageable containers are easier to move, inspect, and isolate. Keep a small immediately accessible amount in the evacuation kit rather than assuming the main household reserve can be carried during a rapid departure.

## Rotation and traceability
Mark each container with the fill date, source, and whether it was commercially sealed or filled at home. When using home-filled storage, sanitize the container correctly and avoid touching the inside of caps or openings. Rotate according to the container or bottled-water manufacturer’s guidance and your local public-health advice. The project’s Water Batch log can track source, handling, container, date, and status so an uncertain batch never gets mixed into accepted water.

## When the normal supply becomes questionable
A pressure loss, flood, damaged main, unusual taste or smell, official advisory, or contamination incident changes the problem from “how much water do we have?” to “which water is acceptable for which use?” Follow the most restrictive current instruction from the water utility or civil-protection/public-health authority. Boiling can kill germs but does not remove many chemicals; if authorities say the supply may contain chemicals, fuel, heavy metals, or other hazardous substances, do not assume boiling makes it safe.

## Reserve failure modes
Common operational failures are storing all water in one inaccessible room, forgetting a manual way to open sealed containers, letting drinking water become the default for every washing task, or using an unlabelled bottle that nobody can trace. After an earthquake or flood, also inspect containers for cracks, dirty caps, damaged seals, and contact with contaminated water before counting them as usable.
