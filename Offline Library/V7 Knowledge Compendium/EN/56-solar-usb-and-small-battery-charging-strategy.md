# Solar, USB and small-battery charging strategy

Small solar panels and power banks can extend communications and lighting during an outage, but poor energy accounting can waste scarce daylight or overheat damaged batteries. Treat charging as a scheduled resource operation rather than a permanent collection of cables.

## Start with the loads

List devices by consequence of failure: medical support and emergency communications first, navigation and essential lighting next, convenience last. Note each device’s connector, approximate energy need, minimum usable charge and whether it can operate while charging. Disable unnecessary background use before buying more batteries.

## Match source, converter and device

Use intact cables and equipment with compatible ratings. Do not improvise bare-wire connections or use swollen, leaking, wet or abnormally hot batteries. Place charging equipment where it stays dry, ventilated and protected from foot traffic. A solar panel should be positioned according to its instructions without creating a fall, roof or electrical hazard.

## Use charging windows

During useful sunlight, charge the storage device or highest-priority load first according to system design. Record starting and ending percentages or watt-hours when available; this reveals whether the setup is actually producing enough energy. At night, stop low-value loads before the reserve falls below the amount assigned to emergency communications or medical needs.

## Plan for failure

Keep one manual fallback for critical functions: paper contacts, printed maps, non-electric medication schedule, mechanical key and low-power light. A battery system is a continuity aid, not a guarantee.

**Source anchors:** manufacturer electrical limits, fire-service battery-safety guidance and Ready.gov outage planning. This chapter does not provide custom wiring instructions.
