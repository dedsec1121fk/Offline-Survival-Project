# Post-Incident Learning Review

## Review the system, not personalities
A useful debrief asks what conditions, information, tools or procedures made an error likely. Avoid turning the review into a search for someone to blame. People will hide near-misses if every mistake becomes a personal accusation.

## Reconstruct the timeline
Use the incident log, decisions, communication windows and field observations to identify what was known at each major decision point. Judge a decision using information available at that time rather than facts learned afterward.

## Classify gaps
Separate missing equipment, insufficient quantity, missing skill, unclear ownership, failed communication, bad assumption and external dependency. Different causes need different corrections. Buying more equipment will not fix an unclear handover, and another checklist will not replace a missing physical spare.

## Capture near-misses
Record events that almost caused harm or loss even if no damage occurred: a nearly depleted battery before an alert, an inaccessible medication bag, a blocked evacuation bag, or two people assuming the other had called a relative. Near-misses are often cheaper lessons than actual failures.

## Turn lessons into owned changes
Every accepted improvement should have an owner, due date and verification method. “Improve communications” is too vague; “print the contact card and run a two-channel check next Saturday” can be completed and tested.

## Retire obsolete material
Delete or archive outdated routes, duplicate checklists and superseded local procedures after the replacement is verified. Preparedness degrades when several conflicting versions remain active and nobody knows which one is current.
