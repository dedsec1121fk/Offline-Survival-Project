# Information Verification During an Outage

## Purpose
When networks are unstable, the dangerous problem is often not a complete lack of information but a mixture of old screenshots, rumors, partial observations and official messages that refer to different places or times. Build a small verification routine before the incident so the household can separate what is known, what is plausible and what still needs confirmation.

## Keep a source ladder
Write down the official channels relevant to your area before connectivity becomes unreliable: civil-protection alerts, local authorities, utility notices, emergency radio, and any building or community coordinator you actually trust. For every important claim, record the source, time received, geographic area and whether it changes an action. A message that is authentic but six hours old may be less useful than a newer local instruction.

## Separate observation from interpretation
A field observation should be literal: “smoke visible north of the road at 18:20” is different from “the fire is moving toward us.” The second statement is an inference unless confirmed by a competent source. Keep those two categories separate in the incident log so later decisions can be reconstructed without turning assumptions into facts.

## Work with conflicting reports
Do not average conflicting safety instructions. Pause non-urgent movement, identify which source has authority for the exact hazard and location, and seek a newer confirmation through a different channel. If an official evacuation or protective action is issued for your location, follow it rather than waiting for social-media consensus.

## Low-bandwidth discipline
Prefer short text messages, saved screenshots with visible timestamps, and written radio notes over repeated calls or large media files. Cache critical official instructions locally when possible. During a prolonged outage, nominate one person per shift to update the common log so everyone is not draining batteries while checking the same rumor.

## Audit questions
At each handover ask: Which facts changed? Which claims remain unverified? Which source is authoritative for the next decision? What information would cause us to change route, shelter, water, power or medical-continuity actions? Delete or mark superseded information instead of leaving contradictory instructions side by side.
