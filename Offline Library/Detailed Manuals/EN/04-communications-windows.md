# Communications windows and message-discipline manual

## Why windows matter
During congestion or battery shortage, continuous calling wastes power and creates uncertainty when someone does not answer immediately. A communications window defines who listens or checks, when, by which method, on which channel or address, and what happens after a missed contact. The schedule should be simple enough to reconstruct from paper.

## Primary and fallback paths
A primary method might be mobile data, voice, SMS, an agreed radio channel, a landline, an in-person meeting point, or a message relayed through a trusted contact. The backup should fail differently. Two messaging applications on the same phone and network are not strong redundancy if the phone, tower or battery is the single point of failure.

## Message structure
Put the decision-relevant fact first: status, location at an appropriate level of precision, immediate need, movement intention, and next expected contact. Do not fill scarce airtime with a long narrative before saying that someone is injured or that a route is blocked. Use agreed plain language; emergency responders and untrained family members should not need to decode invented tactical jargon.

## Missed window procedure
Decide in advance what one missed check means and what two or more misses mean. The response may be “wait until the next window,” “try the fallback,” “contact the out-of-area person,” or “start a welfare check if conditions permit.” The correct escalation depends on the hazard and household, so record it rather than assuming everybody shares the same expectation.

## Privacy
A working radio or group chat is not automatically private. Limit unnecessary medical details, exact cache locations, door codes, financial information and identity data. If precise coordinates are needed for rescue, send them to the appropriate trusted recipient, not automatically to every participant.

## Network discipline
Greece’s Civil Protection guidance for earthquakes notes that unnecessary phone use can overload networks. Short messages and scheduled check-ins reduce repeated calling. When 112 or another emergency service is needed, emergency communication takes priority over the household schedule.
