# Long-outage kitchen and cooking-priority manual

## Decide what the kitchen is trying to achieve
During a long outage the kitchen has four competing jobs: keep food safe, provide enough usable meals, conserve water, and avoid fire or carbon-monoxide hazards. A fifth job is cleanup. A meal that consumes more safe water and washing capacity than the household can replace may not be the best use of supplies even if ingredients are available.

## Sequence food use
Start with food that is genuinely safe and most perishable, then opened shelf-stable products, then intact long-storage food. Do not use this sequence to justify eating refrigeration-dependent food with an unsafe temperature history. The cold-chain decision comes first.

## Cooking energy
Compare meals by total energy required, not just burner time. Heating a large mass of water repeatedly may consume more fuel than a meal that needs a short boil or no cooking. If using an electric cooker from stored battery power, include its watt-hours in the Power Operations load sheet rather than treating cooking as “free” electricity.

## Combustion equipment
Never move a charcoal grill, camping stove or generator into the living space because weather is bad outside. Carbon monoxide is invisible and odorless. Use only appliances in the manner and location specified by their manufacturer and current fire/public-health guidance.

## Water and dishwashing
Use clean utensils and prevent raw or contaminated food from touching ready-to-eat items. When water is scarce, simplify the menu and number of utensils instead of reducing hygiene randomly. Keep drinking-water containers separate from dishwater and chemical storage.

## Meal record
A short meal log can show what stock is disappearing faster than expected, which foods people are refusing, and whether special diets are being missed. This matters in a multi-day event because a mathematically adequate pantry can still fail if its contents cannot be safely prepared or actually eaten.
