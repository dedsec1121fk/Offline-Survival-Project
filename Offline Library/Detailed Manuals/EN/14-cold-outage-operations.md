# Cold-weather outage operations manual

## Protect people before pipes and property
In a winter outage, the first objective is maintaining safe body temperature and preventing carbon-monoxide or fire hazards from improvised heating. Move people into the smallest practical safe living zone, use dry layers and bedding, and monitor those who may not perceive or communicate cold stress reliably.

## Heating choices
Do not use a gas oven to heat the home. CDC explicitly warns that this can cause carbon-monoxide poisoning. Generators, charcoal equipment and other combustion devices belong outdoors according to current safety guidance. If using a rated indoor heater, maintain its required clearances and power/fuel conditions.

## Concentrate operations
Choose one warm operating zone for sleep, communication and essential care if this can be done safely. Close unused spaces without blocking exits. Keep charging, candles or heaters away from bedding and clutter. Battery lighting is preferable to open flames where practical.

## Water-system awareness
Freezing can damage pipes, tanks, pumps and filters. Know which parts of the property are vulnerable and how the normal shutoff works before the emergency. Do not use open flame to thaw frozen plumbing. If a leak appears after thawing, isolate water only if the control can be reached safely.

## Vehicle risk
A vehicle can provide temporary shelter or charging but brings exhaust, fuel and travel hazards. Never run an engine in an enclosed garage. Keep snow or debris from blocking the exhaust. Severe weather may make staying put safer than attempting a road journey; follow official travel guidance.

## Operational checks
At each check-in record indoor condition, vulnerable occupants, heating method, ventilation/CO alarm status, available power, water condition and the next trigger for changing shelter. This turns a long cold night into a sequence of reviewed decisions.
