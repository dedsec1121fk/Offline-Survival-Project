# Pet and service-animal continuity manual

## Animals are part of the evacuation system
A pet plan needs identification, carrier or lead, food, water, waste supplies, medication, veterinary information and a destination that will accept the animal. A service animal additionally supports a person’s function; separating the pair may create an accessibility emergency even if the animal itself is physically safe.

## Handling under stress
Fire alarms, earthquakes, crowds and unfamiliar shelters can change animal behavior. Keep carriers reachable rather than buried behind stored supplies. Practise calm loading before an emergency. Do not open a carrier in an unsecured area because an animal that is normally calm can bolt after a frightening event.

## Food and water
Track animal food separately from human food so it is not accidentally consumed from the wrong planning horizon. Note any prescription diet or medication exactly as supplied by the veterinarian. Do not improvise human medication doses for an animal.

## Identification
Keep current contact information on the animal where appropriate and maintain a recent photograph showing the animal with the owner or handler. Ready.gov recommends including animals in emergency plans and preparing both shelter-in-place and portable supplies.

## Heat, smoke and contaminated ground
Animals can be affected by heat, smoke, debris, chemicals and floodwater. Protect paws and prevent drinking from unknown standing water. If the environment is unsafe for people, it is generally not a suitable holding area for animals either.

## Assigned responsibility
Name one person to load each animal and a backup if possible. During evacuation, record that the animal has actually been loaded; do not rely on “I thought you had the cat.” For a service animal, also record essential working equipment and what support the handler needs if the animal cannot work temporarily.
