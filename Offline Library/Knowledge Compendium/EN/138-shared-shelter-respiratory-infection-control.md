# Shared Shelter Respiratory Illness: Separation, Ventilation, and Handover

Crowded evacuation shelters combine close contact, shared toilets, stress, disrupted sleep, and people with different health vulnerabilities. A household-level plan cannot run clinical infection control, but it can reduce unnecessary exposure and make symptoms visible to shelter staff early.

## Report illness instead of hiding it
If a household member develops fever, repeated vomiting/diarrhea, significant cough with breathing difficulty, rash with systemic illness, or another concerning symptom, inform shelter staff or medical personnel promptly. Do not silently move the person between sleeping areas. Follow the facility’s separation, masking, hygiene, and medical-evaluation procedures; these may change with the disease and local public-health situation.

## Keep personal items personal
Do not share drinking containers, eating utensils, towels, toothbrushes, medicines, respiratory equipment, or items that contact the face unless they are designed and cleaned for shared use. Assign a small clean area for the ill person’s essentials and a separate bag for laundry/waste. Caregivers should know where hand hygiene is available before beginning food or personal-care tasks.

## Preserve accessibility and dignity
Separation measures must not strand a person from mobility assistance, communication support, service animals, or essential medical devices. Record who remains responsible for care and how the person can summon help. Avoid public labeling that discloses unnecessary medical information; staff need actionable facts, not household gossip.

## Handover what changed
At shift changes, communicate symptoms, staff instructions, last meaningful change, hydration/food concern, equipment needs, and whether the shelter assigned a different area. This reduces duplicated questioning and prevents a new caregiver from unintentionally breaking the facility’s infection-control plan.
