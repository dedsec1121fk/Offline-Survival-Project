# Home-fire escape, smoke and door decisions

A home fire is a time-critical escape problem, not a property-saving exercise. Smoke can make a familiar corridor unusable quickly, and opening the wrong door can expose a room to heat and smoke. Household preparation therefore matters more than improvisation after an alarm.

## Build the escape plan before the fire

Identify two ways out of sleeping areas where feasible, an outdoor meeting point that is away from traffic and responding vehicles, and who assists children, older adults, disabled people or animals without delaying escape. Keep exits usable; do not store emergency supplies where they narrow the only route. Practice the plan in daylight and darkness without creating unsafe drills.

## During an alarm or signs of fire

Leave promptly and follow fire-service guidance. Do not return for phones, documents, pets or equipment if doing so means re-entering danger. If smoke or heat blocks the planned route, use the alternate route rather than testing a visibly hazardous corridor. A closed door can slow smoke and heat; before opening an unfamiliar door in a fire situation, assess conditions and follow official fire-safety training rather than forcing it open.

## Accountability outside

At the meeting point, count people and tell responders immediately who may still be inside, their likely location, mobility needs and any known hazards such as oxygen cylinders, batteries, fuel or animals. Do not send an untrained person back in.

## After extinguishment

Do not re-enter merely because visible flames are gone. Fire can leave hot spots, toxic smoke residues, structural damage, wet electrical systems and contaminated food or medicines. Wait for clearance and record damage from a safe area.

**Source anchors:** Ready.gov home-fire guidance and local fire-service instructions. Install and maintain smoke alarms according to local rules and manufacturer guidance.
