# Infant feeding during water, power, and evacuation emergencies

## Protect the feeding method that already works
An emergency can remove safe water, refrigeration, electricity, privacy, cleaning facilities, transport, or access to normal supplies at the same time. The safest plan starts before the disruption: record how the infant is normally fed, what equipment is genuinely required, where replacement supplies can be obtained, and who can help if the primary caregiver is separated or exhausted. Do not change feeding methods during an emergency merely because a donated product is available.

## Breastfeeding usually removes several infrastructure dependencies
Where breastfeeding is already established, maintaining it can avoid the need for mixing water, sterilizing preparation equipment, or preserving prepared formula. A displaced breastfeeding caregiver still needs safe drinking water, food, rest, privacy where possible, and access to skilled feeding support if pain, illness, stress, separation, or supply concerns arise. An emergency plan should identify where that support can be reached rather than assuming the caregiver must solve feeding problems alone.

## Formula feeding becomes a water-and-hygiene operation
CDC emergency guidance identifies ready-to-feed liquid infant formula as the safest formula option in an emergency because it does not need water for mixing and is packaged for direct use. If powdered or concentrated formula is the only available option, follow current public-health and product instructions and use water that authorities identify as safe for infant feeding. Do not dilute formula to make it last longer, and do not improvise homemade formula.

## Cleaning capacity matters as much as the formula supply
Bottles, nipples, cups, pump parts, hands, work surfaces, and storage containers can become contamination routes when water is limited. Keep a dedicated clean feeding zone, preserve single-use items for situations where cleaning is difficult, and separate dirty items from prepared feeds. If safe cleaning cannot be maintained, seek public-health, shelter, maternity, pediatric, or feeding-support assistance early rather than stretching a failing process.

## Power loss changes expressed-milk and prepared-feed decisions
Cold storage should be managed according to current CDC breast-milk/formula storage guidance and the actual temperature history, not by smell or appearance. Minimize unnecessary opening of refrigerators/freezers. If relocation is likely, plan an insulated transport method and confirm cold-storage access at the destination. Record when power was lost, when milk/feed moved to another cold source, and any uncertainty so the caregiver can make the next decision from evidence rather than memory.

## Escalate feeding difficulty early
An infant who is feeding poorly, producing fewer wet diapers than expected, unusually sleepy, repeatedly vomiting, or otherwise appears unwell needs prompt medical assessment. The offline project should support continuity and records; it does not diagnose dehydration, prescribe feeding volumes, or replace pediatric care.
