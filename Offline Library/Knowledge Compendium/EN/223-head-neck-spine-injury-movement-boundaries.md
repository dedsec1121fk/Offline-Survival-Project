# Head, Neck, and Spine Injury: Movement Boundaries

A fall, collision, diving incident, collapse, or blow to the head can injure the spine even when the person is awake and talking. The field priority is not to diagnose a spinal injury; it is to avoid unnecessary movement while protecting breathing, controlling immediate threats, and getting professional help.

## Suspect a serious injury from mechanism and signs
Be cautious after high-energy falls, vehicle collisions, diving accidents, crushing events, or significant blows to the head/neck. Warning signs include severe neck/back pain, weakness, numbness, tingling, loss of movement, unusual limb position, altered consciousness, loss of bladder/bowel control, or breathing problems. A person may also have no obvious deformity.

## Keep the person where they are when the scene is safe
Ask them not to move their head or neck. Support the head in the position found with your hands or stable padding if that can be done without forcing alignment. Do not twist, straighten, sit up, or walk the person merely to make them more comfortable. Avoid removing a helmet unless it obstructs breathing or trained responders need it removed.

## Movement can still become necessary
Immediate hazards change the balance. Fire, rising water, traffic, structural collapse, or an inability to maintain the airway may make movement unavoidable. Use the minimum movement needed to escape the hazard and keep the head/neck as stable as practical. If the person becomes unresponsive, breathing and circulation take priority over perfect spinal immobilization.

## Monitor until handoff
Track consciousness, breathing, skin condition, movement and sensation in hands/feet, increasing pain, vomiting, seizures, or deterioration. Prevent heat loss without overheating. Do not give food or drink if significant trauma may require urgent procedures.

## Handoff information
Record the mechanism, approximate height/speed if known, time, loss of consciousness, observed movement/sensation, vomiting/seizures, medicines such as anticoagulants if known, and any movement that was necessary before responders arrived.
