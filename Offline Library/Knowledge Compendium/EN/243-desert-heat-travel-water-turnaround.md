# Desert Heat Travel: Water, Pace, Shade, and Turnaround Decisions

In extreme heat, distance alone does not describe travel difficulty. Radiant heat, dry air, elevation, wind, pack weight, acclimatization and water availability can turn a normal route into a medical emergency. The plan should make heat avoidance the first control, not “toughness.”

## Shift movement away from peak heat
When safe and compatible with local hazards, schedule strenuous travel for cooler hours and use shade/rest during the hottest period. Check official heat warnings before departure. A route with no shade, no reliable water and no bailout option needs a much more conservative turnaround point.

## Carry and track water; do not ration into illness
Start hydrated and carry a realistic reserve for the specific people, temperature and route. Record consumption rather than discovering late that the group used most of the supply early. Do not deliberately withhold water from a symptomatic person to “make it last.” Heat illness requires cooling and appropriate medical action.

## Pace to the least heat-tolerant member
Children, older adults, people with illness/medicines, unacclimatized travelers and anyone carrying a heavy load may reach limits sooner. Frequent short rests in shade can be more effective than a long rest after exhaustion. Watch coordination, behavior, headache, nausea, weakness and sweating changes.

## Turnaround must preserve the return
Do not use half the water merely to reach the halfway distance if the return is hotter, uphill or more exposed. Define a time, water reserve and heat-condition threshold that forces retreat. If the route has become uncertain, solve navigation before spending more water on forward movement.

## Heat stroke is an emergency
Altered mental status, collapse, seizure or severe overheating requires immediate emergency action and rapid cooling while help is activated. Do not wait for the person to “push through.”
