# Eye Chemical Splash and Eye-Injury Boundaries

Eye emergencies are especially unforgiving of delay. During cleanup, battery work, fuel handling, bleach use, dust/debris removal, or workshop repair, an eye exposure may occur when normal medical access is already disrupted.

## Chemical splash: rinse immediately
For a chemical splashed into the eye, begin rinsing with plentiful clean, room-temperature water immediately. Poison Control guidance emphasizes irrigating first rather than spending time identifying every ingredient. Remove contact lenses if they come out easily and keep water moving across the eye while the person blinks. Continue for the duration advised by the product safety guidance/poison service; many poison-control protocols use at least 15–20 minutes for eye irrigation.

## Do not neutralize chemicals in the eye
Do not add vinegar, baking soda, bleach, alcohol, antiseptic, or another chemical to “neutralize” the exposure. Do not rub the eye. If a solid particle is embedded, an object penetrated the eye, or trauma caused visible deformity, protect the eye from pressure and obtain emergency care rather than attempting removal.

## Escalation signs
Severe pain, persistent redness/tearing, visual change, light sensitivity, inability to open the eye, chemical burns, or any high-velocity/penetrating injury warrants urgent professional assessment. A splash involving a concentrated industrial cleaner, pesticide, fuel, battery electrolyte, or unknown chemical should be discussed with poison/emergency professionals as soon as possible.

## Prevent the second casualty
The helper should avoid direct contact with the chemical. Remove contaminated gloves/clothing safely, protect the clean water supply, and ventilate if inhalation exposure is also possible. Record the product name/label and time of exposure for responders.
