# Chronic respiratory and cardiac conditions during wildfire-smoke episodes

## Smoke risk continues even when the flame front is distant
Wildfire smoke contains fine particles and other pollutants that can aggravate asthma, COPD, cardiovascular disease and other chronic conditions. CDC notes that some people with chronic disease, children, pregnant people, and older adults may be more vulnerable. A household with a medically vulnerable person should therefore manage smoke as a health-continuity problem, not wait until visible flames approach.

## Start from the clinician’s action plan
Keep prescribed inhalers, spacers, nebulizer equipment if used, heart/lung medicines, oxygen instructions, peak-flow or other monitoring tools if prescribed, and the written action plan together. Verify expiration dates and the power requirements of any nebulizer, concentrator, CPAP/BiPAP, or monitoring device. The project can track those dependencies but should never increase medicine frequency or change oxygen settings on its own.

## Reduce exposure using the cleanest practical indoor space
When evacuation is not ordered, close the building against outdoor smoke as appropriate, avoid indoor particle sources, and use compatible filtration/air cleaning if available. If the building cannot maintain tolerable air because of leakage, heat, or power failure, identify a cleaner-air location or public facility before symptoms worsen. A generator must never be moved indoors to power air-cleaning or medical equipment.

## Outdoor tasks need a necessity test
Do not send the medically vulnerable household member outside simply because another person wants a visual update. Combine essential outdoor tasks, use official air-quality/smoke information, and minimize exposure. Respiratory protection can reduce particulate exposure when correctly selected and worn, but it does not make an evacuation route safe from flame, extreme heat, carbon monoxide, or all gases.

## Symptoms override the household plan
New or worsening breathing difficulty, chest pain, severe wheezing, fainting, confusion, blue/gray discoloration, or symptoms that do not improve according to the person’s action plan require urgent professional assessment. Follow local emergency-service instructions. If the person’s normal care depends on power or a nearby hospital, smoke plus outage may justify earlier relocation.

## Keep monitoring after visible smoke decreases
CDC notes that smoke can remain after a wildfire is no longer visibly active nearby. Continue checking local air-quality/health instructions and keep the cleaner-air plan ready until conditions genuinely improve. Replace filters and clean ash/dust according to safe post-fire guidance rather than immediately stirring deposits indoors.
