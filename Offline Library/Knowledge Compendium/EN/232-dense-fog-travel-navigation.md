# Dense Fog: Travel and Navigation When Visibility Collapses

Fog turns a familiar road, coast or trail into a short-range navigation problem. The most important decision is often whether movement should continue at all. Speed, stopping distance, route complexity and the possibility of becoming a traffic obstacle matter more than keeping the planned schedule.

## Driving: reduce demand before visibility becomes critical
Slow gradually, increase following distance and use low-beam headlights as appropriate. High beams can reflect from fog and reduce useful visibility. Keep windows and mirrors clear and remove distractions. If visibility becomes too poor to continue safely, use a safe location completely away from the travel lane where possible rather than stopping in an active lane.

## Do not follow lights blindly
The vehicle ahead may be lost, stopped, or leaving the road. Maintain independent lane/road awareness using markings and known route information without staring at taillights. Avoid sudden lane changes and overtaking when you cannot see enough distance to confirm the maneuver.

## On foot or in the mountains
Fog can erase landmarks and create accidental cliff, road, water or avalanche exposure. Stop before uncertainty becomes displacement. Record your last confirmed position, compare compass/GPS/offline map information, and consider waiting in a sheltered safe location instead of “walking until something looks familiar.” Keep the group together and use deliberate bearing/distance legs when movement is necessary.

## Coastal and marine edge
Dense fog near roads, cliffs, harbors or shoreline adds moving-vehicle and water hazards. Sound carries unpredictably; hearing a vessel or traffic does not reliably reveal distance. Increase separation from cliff edges, surf zones and roads.

## Log the decision
Record time, approximate visibility, location, route status and whether you stopped or diverted. This is useful when another person must decide whether to follow the same route later.
