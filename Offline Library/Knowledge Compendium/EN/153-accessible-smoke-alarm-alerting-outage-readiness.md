# Accessible Smoke Alarm Alerting for Hearing or Vision Limitations

A smoke alarm is only protective if occupants can perceive it and act. Hearing loss, deep sleep, cognitive impairment, closed doors, background equipment noise, or a power outage can make an otherwise functional alarm ineffective for a particular person.

## Test the alert with the person present
Do not assume an alarm is audible from a bedroom because it is loud in the hall. Use the manufacturer’s test procedure and verify that every occupant can detect the signal in the circumstances in which they sleep. U.S. Fire Administration guidance notes that people who are deaf or hard of hearing may need devices such as strobes, bed shakers, or alternative audible signals.

## Protect backup power and placement
Record battery type/replacement date or sealed-alarm replacement date, test schedule, and which alarms depend on building power. Never remove batteries to silence nuisance alarms. If an alerting accessory uses a separate receiver, charger, or bedside unit, include that device in the outage power budget.

## Link alerting to evacuation assistance
An accessible alarm does not solve a blocked route or mobility limitation. Pair the alert method with an escape plan, helper/back-up helper, doors/exits that fit mobility equipment, and a meeting point. Test the entire chain during drills.
