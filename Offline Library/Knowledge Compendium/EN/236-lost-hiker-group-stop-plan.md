# Lost Hiker or Group: Stop, Stabilize, and Build a Searchable Position

When a group realizes it is lost, continued random movement can turn a navigation error into exposure, injury, or a much larger search area. The first useful action is to stop the drift, preserve a known position, and decide whether self-recovery is genuinely safer than staying put.

## Stop before guessing a direction
Bring the whole group together. Confirm the last location that was known with confidence, current time, remaining daylight, weather, injuries, water, clothing, batteries and whether anyone is missing. Do not send individuals in different directions to “look around.” That creates more people to account for and destroys the group’s last common point.

## Observe without committing to movement
Compare the offline map, compass, terrain, trail signs, GPS coordinates if available, elevation clues and recent travel direction. Listen for roads, water or human activity, but do not treat a sound as a reliable bearing by itself. If you can safely gain a short local view without losing the current point, mark the point and keep visual/voice contact.

## Decide: self-rescue or stay searchable
Self-rescue makes sense only when the route back to a confirmed feature is clear, conditions are stable, the group can move safely and the move will not cross a known hazard. Staying put is often better after dark, in poor visibility, with injury, when a search has likely begun, or when every proposed route is based on guesswork.

## Make the position easier to find
Use high-contrast visible material, a whistle, flashlight, reflective surface, or safe open location. Conserve phone power by using airplane/low-power mode between planned checks when network behavior permits. If a message gets through, send coordinates, number of people, condition, clothing/shelter, planned action and the time of the message.

## Manage the night, not the ego
Insulation from wind and ground, dry layers, water protection and group warmth can be more important than covering another kilometer. Keep a written timeline of moves and decisions so rescuers can understand what happened if communication resumes.
