# River and Stream Crossings: Turnaround Boundaries

Moving water is difficult to judge from the bank. A crossing that was easy in the morning can become dangerous after rain, snowmelt or upstream release. The safest crossing decision is often to wait, turn around, or find a bridge rather than prove the group can wade.

## Treat uncertainty as a reason to stop
Do not enter icy, flood-stage, fast, white, opaque, or depth-uncertain water. Avoid crossings near waterfalls, strainers, culverts, steep downstream drops, debris jams or places where a fall would immediately sweep a person into a hazard. If you cannot see or confidently assess the bottom and downstream recovery area, the risk is already elevated.

## Check whether crossing is actually necessary
Look for an official bridge, maintained trail crossing, upstream/downstream alternative, or a route that avoids the water entirely. Compare the time cost of a detour with the consequence of one person falling while carrying a pack. Emergency schedules do not make hydraulics safer.

## If a shallow crossing is chosen under appropriate conditions
Unbuckle pack waist/chest straps when doing so will allow quick release, secure loose gear, use stable footwear and cross one controlled step at a time. Avoid jumping between slippery rocks with a heavy pack. Keep group members positioned so one person's fall does not pull others down.

## After heavy rain or snowmelt
Wait and reassess. Streams can rise rapidly and may fall again after the peak passes. Do not rely on yesterday's depth or an old map description. Cold water adds hypothermia and loss-of-function risk even when the current is not extreme.

## Abort early
If water rises above the planned boundary, footing repeatedly shifts, a member becomes frightened or cold, or the current moves legs unexpectedly, back out while control remains. Turning around is a successful safety decision, not a failed crossing.
