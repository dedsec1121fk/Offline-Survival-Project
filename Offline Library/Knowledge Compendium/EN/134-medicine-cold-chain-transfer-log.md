# Medicine Cold-Chain Transfer: Evidence, Handover, and Escalation

Temperature-sensitive medicine cannot be managed safely by a household rule such as “it still feels cold.” Different products have different storage ranges, excursion allowances, light protection, freezing sensitivity, and disposal/replacement rules. The purpose of an emergency cold-chain log is to preserve the history needed for a pharmacist, manufacturer, or clinician to make a product-specific decision.

## Start the clock when conditions change
Record the last known normal refrigeration time, outage start, refrigerator temperature if measured, each container transfer, the cold source used, and every period when temperature is unknown. Keep medicines physically separated from melting ice or freezing elements if the product guidance requires it. Do not freeze a medicine merely because “colder is safer.”

## Move information with the medicine
The transfer pack should include the medicine name, strength/form, lot/expiry when visible, patient name, prescription details, storage instructions, and the contact route for the pharmacy or care team. If the medicine changes hands, record who received it and when. This prevents a later caregiver from assuming uninterrupted refrigeration when a period was actually unknown.

## Do not guess whether an excursion is acceptable
If storage conditions leave the labeled range, contact the pharmacist, manufacturer, or treating team for that specific product. Avoid changing doses, skipping essential therapy, or discarding a critical supply solely because of a generic internet rule. If the medicine is visibly damaged, contaminated by floodwater, or its packaging integrity is compromised, isolate it and seek product-specific advice.

## Recovery action
After stable power returns, reconcile all temperature-sensitive medicines: which remained usable, which require replacement, what backup equipment failed, and whether the reserve plan needs more cold packs, a monitored cooler, or an earlier relocation trigger.
