# Whiteout and Snow Navigation: The Stop Rule

Whiteout removes contrast, horizon and terrain cues. A person can walk off route, into avalanche terrain, onto thin ice or over an edge while believing they are moving straight. The critical survival skill is recognizing when navigation quality has fallen below the level required for safe movement.

## Define a stop rule before visibility disappears
Examples include losing the next known landmark, being unable to confirm direction with two independent cues, losing sight/voice contact with the group, or no longer distinguishing terrain hazards within stopping distance. A stop rule prevents “just another hundred meters” from becoming uncontrolled displacement.

## Preserve the last known point
Record coordinates, elevation, time, compass bearing, recent direction and the feature last confirmed. Mark the location physically if appropriate. Do not allow everyone to wander in search of a trail. If a scout move is absolutely necessary, use a very short controlled tether/visual return method appropriate to the environment rather than unbounded exploration.

## Navigation tools have different failure modes
A compass can provide direction but not reveal cliffs or avalanche slopes. GPS can show position but may have stale maps, low battery or poor interpretation. Track files can be inaccurate. Combine tools with terrain knowledge and current conditions rather than treating any display as permission to continue.

## Shelter may be the correct navigation action
If movement cannot be made safely, protect from wind and cold, make the position conspicuous, conserve batteries and communicate coordinates/status when possible. Avoid settling in an avalanche runout, cornice zone, frozen water surface, drainage channel or directly below loaded trees.

## Restart deliberately
When visibility improves, verify position again before moving. Do not assume the direction faced after hours of shelter is the original travel direction.
