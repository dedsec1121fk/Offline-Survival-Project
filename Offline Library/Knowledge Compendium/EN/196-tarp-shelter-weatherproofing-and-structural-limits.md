# Tarp Shelter: Weatherproofing and Structural Limits

A tarp can block rain, wind and sun, but it is not a substitute for a structurally safe building when severe weather, falling debris, wildfire, flood or lightning hazards are present. Site selection is more important than a clever knot.

## Choose the site before the shape
Avoid dry stream channels, flood paths, unstable slopes, dead trees/branches, cliff/rockfall zones, shorelines exposed to surge, and places where vehicles cannot see you. Look uphill and upwind for hazards. In thunderstorms, a tarp does not make an exposed ridge or isolated tall-tree site safe.

## Manage water
Pitch so rain runs away from the sleeping area. Keep the tarp edge from funneling water under the groundsheet. Do not dig drainage trenches where excavation is prohibited or would worsen erosion; choose naturally drained ground instead. Keep bedding insulated from ground moisture.

## Manage wind load
Lower profiles and more anchor points generally reduce flapping and point loads. Use attachment methods that spread force instead of concentrating it on one fragile grommet. Inspect anchors as wind changes. Do not place heavy rocks or improvised spikes where a failure can launch them into people.

## Ventilation and fire
Do not seal a small tarp shelter around combustion appliances. Never operate a generator, charcoal device or fuel-burning stove inside or immediately adjacent to an enclosed shelter. Keep flames/sparks away from synthetic fabric.

## Exit criteria
If water rises, anchors repeatedly fail, trees/structures become unstable, smoke/heat approaches or authorities order evacuation, abandon the shelter. Protecting the tarp is never the priority over moving people.
