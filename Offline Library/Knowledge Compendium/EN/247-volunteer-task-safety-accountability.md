# Volunteer Task Safety and Accountability During Community Response

Spontaneous volunteers can multiply useful capacity after a disaster, but unassigned work can also create injuries, duplicated effort and missing-person confusion. A small accountability system keeps people useful without pretending they are professional responders.

## Match the task to competence
Ask what the person can safely do, what equipment they have, and what they should not do. Suitable tasks may include information delivery, supply sorting, low-risk welfare checks, charging coordination, translation, meal distribution, documentation or moving light materials. Structural rescue, live electrical work, gas repair, hazardous-material cleanup and clinical procedures require appropriate qualifications and authority.

## Every task needs five fields
Record the worker or pair, task, exact area, start time and expected return/report time. Add the person responsible for closing the task. A verbal “go help over there” instruction is difficult to account for when conditions change.

## Brief hazards before departure
Mention known road closures, damaged structures, aggressive animals, heat/cold exposure, protective equipment, hygiene requirements and communication method. Set a hard stop for darkness, weather, fatigue or loss of contact. Volunteers should know that stopping an unsafe task is expected behavior.

## Check back in
Returning volunteers report completion, unresolved hazards, injuries/near misses, supplies consumed and information that changes the situation picture. If a team misses its expected return, attempt contact and escalate according to the plan rather than assuming it decided to stay longer.

## Protect fatigue and judgment
Rotate demanding tasks, provide water/rest/food appropriate to conditions and avoid repeatedly assigning the same skilled person until exhaustion. A fatigued volunteer can become the next casualty or make unsafe technical decisions.
