# Avalanche Terrain: Avoidance, Group Exposure, and Rescue Readiness

Avalanche safety cannot be reduced to a slope-angle number or a phone app. Snow stability changes with weather, wind and terrain, while consequences change with cliffs, gullies, trees and terrain traps. Formal avalanche education and current local forecasts are essential for people who deliberately enter avalanche terrain.

## Avoid exposure when uncertain
The strongest field control is choosing terrain that is not exposed to avalanche start zones, paths or runout. Avalanches can affect gentler terrain below steeper slopes. If you do not have the training to recognize the terrain and current hazard, choose a route that avoids it rather than trying to “read” the snow during an emergency trip.

## Group movement matters
Do not cluster the entire group in one exposed area. Identify safer regroup points outside runout/terrain traps and keep visual/communication accountability. A single person crossing at a time can reduce the number exposed, but this is not a substitute for choosing safe terrain.

## Rescue equipment requires practice
Beacon, probe and shovel are not magic equipment; they require compatible devices, correct wear and repeated practice. People traveling in avalanche terrain should know how to perform a companion rescue and should not assume cellular rescue will arrive immediately.
