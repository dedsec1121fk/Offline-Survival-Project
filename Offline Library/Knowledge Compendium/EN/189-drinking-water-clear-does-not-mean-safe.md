# Drinking Water: Clear Does Not Mean Safe

Water can look, smell and taste normal while containing disease-causing microorganisms or chemicals. Sensory checks are useful for detecting some obvious problems, but they are not a safety test.

## Use a source hierarchy
Prefer sealed commercial water or a public supply that authorities say is safe. Next use water you stored safely before the incident. Treat uncertain sources only with a method appropriate to the suspected hazard. A filter designed for sediment or microorganisms may not remove dissolved chemicals; boiling kills many pathogens but does not remove many chemicals and can concentrate some contaminants as water evaporates.

## Let the advisory determine the action
A boil-water advisory, do-not-drink advisory and do-not-use advisory mean different things. Keep the exact wording and issuing authority in the incident log. Do not convert every advisory into “boil it” by habit. If authorities suspect chemical contamination, boiling can be the wrong response.

## Prevent recontamination
Treatment is only half of safe-water practice. Store treated water in a clean, covered container, use a clean dispensing method, and keep hands/utensils out of the bulk supply. Mark treatment time and batch when multiple containers are in rotation.

## Watch vulnerable users
Infants, pregnant people, older adults and people with weakened immune systems can face greater consequences from unsafe water. When guidance provides stricter instructions for a vulnerable person, use those instructions rather than the household default.
