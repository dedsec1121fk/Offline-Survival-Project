# Shared shelter sleeping and privacy zones: organize space before fatigue creates conflict

## Divide the room by function
A crowded shelter works better when sleeping, eating, medical/support, charging, animal and waste areas are visibly separated. Keep exits, extinguishers, fire doors and staff routes clear. Do not create private spaces by hanging material over equipment, heaters or emergency signs.

## Build a quiet-sleep period
Agree on lower-light and lower-noise hours while preserving required safety lighting. Assign phone calls, charging conversations and supply sorting away from sleeping rows. Families with infants, night medical needs or irregular work shifts may need a designated area rather than repeated conflict with the main sleep zone.

## Protect personal boundaries
Use simple visual barriers where the facility allows them, but maintain ventilation and line-of-travel requirements. Never require someone to disclose a diagnosis to request a quieter, accessible or more private arrangement. Staff/coordinators need only the information necessary to meet the operational need.

## Keep critical items reachable
Glasses, hearing equipment, mobility aids, medicines and shoes should have a consistent location beside the person without obstructing paths. High-value documents and electronics remain secured rather than left in common charging areas without oversight.

## Plan nighttime accountability
Know who is expected in the shelter and where dependents are sleeping. If someone leaves at night, record destination and expected return when practical. Children should not move through unfamiliar shared facilities alone.

## Reset the space each morning
Remove trip hazards, air/dry bedding where possible, clean high-contact areas according to facility guidance, empty waste and restore emergency routes. A shelter that is not reset gradually becomes harder and less safe to operate.
