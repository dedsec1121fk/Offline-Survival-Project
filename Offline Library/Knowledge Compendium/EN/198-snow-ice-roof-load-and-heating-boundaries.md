# Snow and Ice: Roof Load, Access, and Heating Boundaries

Heavy snow and ice can damage roofs, awnings, solar arrays and temporary shelters while simultaneously making ladder work and travel more dangerous. The survival objective is not to clear every surface; it is to identify unsafe loading and protect people without creating a fall or collapse casualty.

## Watch for structural warning signs
New sagging, unusual cracking/popping sounds, doors suddenly binding, ceiling deformation, leaks or visible movement can indicate distress. Leave an unsafe area and seek professional/emergency guidance rather than sending someone onto the roof. A roof already stressed by snow is not a safe work platform simply because it normally supports people.

## Work from safer positions
When local guidance recommends snow removal, use ground-based tools where practical and avoid damaging roofing or overhead electrical equipment. Keep people out of the fall zone. Do not use ladders on ice or improvise roof access during high wind. Consider where falling snow/ice will land before pulling it down.

## Avoid dangerous thawing
Do not use open flames or improvised high-heat devices to melt roof/pipe ice. Fuel-burning heaters must be used only in the location and ventilation conditions for which they are designed. Carbon-monoxide risk increases when people close buildings tightly during cold outages.

## Solar/utility awareness
Snow-covered photovoltaic panels and conductors can still present electrical hazards. Treat damaged service lines and downed conductors as energized and keep clear.
