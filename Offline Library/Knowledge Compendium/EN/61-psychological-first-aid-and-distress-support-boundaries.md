# Psychological first aid and distress-support boundaries

People in a disaster may be frightened, silent, angry, confused, grieving or intensely practical. Support does not require forcing them to describe the event. Psychological first aid focuses on humane, practical assistance, connection and referral while respecting dignity and choice.

## Start with safety and basic needs

Move the conversation away from immediate physical danger. Offer simple information about shelter, water, food, family contact, medicines or transport. Ask what the person needs now rather than assuming that emotional discussion is the priority. Use clear language and an interpreter or communication support when needed.

## Listen without interrogation

Let the person decide whether to talk. Avoid demanding a detailed retelling, making promises you cannot keep or telling someone exactly how they “should” feel. For a child, maintain connection with a safe caregiver wherever possible and keep explanations appropriate to age and development.

## Restore small choices

Disasters remove control. Offer manageable choices: where to sit, whom to contact first, whether they want water, whether they prefer written or spoken information. Protect privacy and do not turn someone’s distress into a group story or social-media update.

## Know the escalation boundary

Immediate danger to self or others, severe disorientation, inability to care for essential needs, acute medical symptoms or worsening mental-health symptoms require qualified professional/emergency support. Volunteers should work within training and local referral systems.

## Support the helper too

Rotate demanding roles, debrief operational facts without pressuring emotional disclosure, eat/drink/rest when possible and watch for degraded judgement. A helper who is exhausted is also part of the safety system.

**Source anchors:** WHO/IASC psychological first-aid and emergency psychosocial principles; local health and emergency referral pathways.
