# Animal Bites: Rabies, Tetanus, and Medical Follow-Up

An animal bite is both a wound and an exposure event. The immediate problem is bleeding and contamination; the less visible problem is whether rabies, tetanus, deep-tissue injury, or infection requires prompt professional care. Do not let a small-looking puncture create false reassurance.

## Make the scene safe first
Move away from the animal without chasing, trapping, or handling it. If the animal is owned and can be identified safely, record the owner/contact details and the animal description. For wildlife, unknown dogs or cats, bats, or an animal behaving abnormally, avoid direct contact and notify the appropriate local animal/public-health authority. Do not risk another bite to capture the animal.

## Clean the wound early
When severe bleeding is not the immediate priority, wash the bite thoroughly with clean running water and soap. Remove visible dirt without aggressive digging. Cover with a clean dressing. If bleeding is heavy, use direct pressure and follow the severe-bleeding chapter. Bites to the hand, face, genitals, over joints, or deep punctures deserve a lower threshold for medical evaluation because important structures can be injured beneath a small opening.

## Record the exposure
Write down the time, location, animal species, whether it was owned or wild, vaccination information if reliably known, where the bite occurred on the body, and what wound care was performed. Photograph the wound only if doing so does not delay care. This record helps a clinician or public-health service decide whether rabies post-exposure treatment or a tetanus booster is needed.

## Escalate promptly when risk is higher
Seek urgent medical advice for uncontrolled bleeding, loss of movement or sensation, a deep/crushing bite, bites to high-risk locations, immunocompromised people, or signs of infection such as increasing redness, swelling, warmth, pus, fever, or worsening pain. Potential rabies exposure should be assessed promptly; do not wait for symptoms. Rabies prevention is time-sensitive and must be guided by professionals.

## Avoid harmful improvisation
Do not cauterize a bite, apply caustic chemicals, close a contaminated puncture with household glue, or rely on antibiotics left over from another illness. Do not assume an animal is safe because the wound is small.
