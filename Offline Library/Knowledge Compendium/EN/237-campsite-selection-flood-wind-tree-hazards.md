# Campsite Selection: Flood, Wind, Tree, Fire, and Terrain Hazards

An emergency camp should solve exposure without creating a new hazard. Site selection matters more than making a shelter elaborate. Before unpacking, look at what water, wind, trees, fire and terrain could do during the entire night—not only what the site looks like now.

## Stay out of water paths
Avoid dry streambeds, narrow washes, low river benches, depressions with poor drainage and obvious flood debris lines. Rain can fall upstream where the sky above you still looks clear. On coasts and lakes, account for tide, waves and changing wind direction rather than placing camp at the current waterline.

## Look above before looking at the ground
Dead limbs, damaged trees, leaning trunks, loose rock, snow/ice load and unstable cliffs can threaten a flat attractive campsite. Wind can increase overnight. Choose a location outside the likely fall zone of suspect trees and away from rockfall channels.

## Separate fire from shelter and fuel
If fire is permitted and necessary, use an established safe site where possible. Keep flame, stove heat and sparks away from tents, dry vegetation, fuel containers and battery equipment. Never use combustion heating or cooking devices in enclosed shelters unless the device is specifically designed and approved for that use; carbon monoxide can accumulate without visible smoke.

## Manage cold from below
Dry ground insulation often matters as much as overhead cover. Use sleeping pads, dry vegetation only where lawful/safe, spare clothing or other insulating layers to reduce heat loss into the ground. Keep a dry sleeping zone separated from wet clothing and cooking tasks.

## Preserve an exit
Do not camp in a location that depends on one narrow escape route vulnerable to flood, wildfire, falling rock or tide. Mark the exit and keep footwear, light and essential gear reachable at night.
