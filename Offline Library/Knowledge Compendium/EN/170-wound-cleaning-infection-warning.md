# Wound Cleaning, Dressing, and Infection Warning Signs After a Disaster

Cuts and punctures are common after earthquakes, floods, storm debris, broken glass, and cleanup. The risk is not only bleeding: dirty water, soil, rusted/contaminated objects, animal waste, and delayed care can increase infection and tetanus concerns.

## Control dangerous bleeding first
Life-threatening bleeding takes priority over detailed cleaning. Use direct pressure and the severe-bleeding methods for which you are trained. Once bleeding is controlled and the wound is appropriate for household first aid, wash hands and use clean/safe water to gently remove visible dirt. Do not pour fuels, bleach, concentrated disinfectants, or caustic substances into tissue.

## Cover and monitor
Use a clean dressing that protects the wound without cutting off circulation. Change it when wet/dirty and inspect the wound. Record date/time and what contaminated the wound if relevant. Increasing redness that spreads, warmth, swelling, pus, worsening pain, fever, red streaks, or systemic illness warrant medical assessment.

## Floodwater and puncture injuries deserve more caution
CDC specifically warns that floodwater may contain sewage, chemicals, sharp objects, and pathogens. A wound exposed to floodwater should be cleaned and professionally evaluated when appropriate. Deep punctures, crush injuries, bites, wounds with retained foreign bodies, and injuries involving tendons/joints/eyes/face/hands can require urgent specialized care even if bleeding looks modest.

## Tetanus status is part of the handoff
Do not guess whether a “rusty object” alone determines tetanus risk. Tell the clinician the wound type/contamination and the person’s known vaccination history; they determine whether a booster or other treatment is needed.
