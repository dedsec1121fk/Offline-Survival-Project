# Household Cleaning Chemicals After a Disaster: Mixing and Ventilation Boundaries

Cleanup after flood, sewage, mold, or illness often increases the amount of bleach, detergent, disinfectant, and other chemicals used in a small space. The emergency itself may also reduce ventilation. A strong-smelling mixture is not a sign that disinfection is “working better”; incompatible products can release dangerous gases or cause burns.

## Use one product plan at a time
Read the product label and use the concentration, contact time, surface compatibility, PPE, and ventilation it specifies. Never mix bleach with ammonia or other cleaners. Do not pour leftover products into unlabelled drink bottles or combine partial containers to save space. Keep chemicals out of the food/water zone and where children or confused occupants cannot access them.

## Ventilation is part of the task
If the label calls for ventilation, make sure fresh air can enter without creating another immediate hazard such as wildfire smoke, a chemical plume, or unsafe outdoor conditions. Stop if occupants develop eye/throat irritation, dizziness, breathing difficulty, or other concerning symptoms and move to clean air. Do not continue because “the room is almost finished.”

## Disinfection does not replace removal
Mud, sewage, moldy porous materials, and flood-damaged debris often require physical removal and drying; simply adding more disinfectant does not solve trapped moisture or structural contamination. Keep a record of what was cleaned, discarded, and still needs professional assessment.
