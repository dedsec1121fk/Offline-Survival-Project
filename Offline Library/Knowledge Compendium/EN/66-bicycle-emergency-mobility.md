# Bicycle emergency mobility: useful redundancy with strict terrain and load limits

## A bicycle can bypass fuel shortages, not every hazard
Bicycles can extend range when roads are congested or fuel is scarce, but they are poor choices in deep floodwater, heavy smoke, severe wind, icy roads or routes requiring hazardous highway travel. Treat the bicycle as one transport option and define conditions that cancel its use.

## Maintain the simple failure points
Before emergencies, check tires, brakes, chain/drivetrain, lights, reflectors and the fit of racks or bags. Keep the correct pump, basic repair items and any proprietary tool the bicycle genuinely needs. An untested repair kit is weight, not capability.

## Carry load low and secure
Avoid bags swinging from handlebars or loose items that affect steering. Water is dense; distribute it within the bicycle/rider capacity and leave room for safe control. A trailer or cargo bicycle changes turning/braking and must be practiced before relying on it.

## Plan visibility and communication
Night or smoke reduces visibility. Use legally required lighting/reflectors and high-visibility placement where appropriate. Keep a small offline route card rather than handling a phone while riding. Stop in a safe place to check messages or navigation.

## Pair riders deliberately
A child, novice rider or person with a mechanical problem can reduce group speed sharply. Establish regroup points and never leave someone alone because another rider can travel faster. If one bicycle carries medical or communication equipment, have a transfer plan if that bike fails.

## Inspect after hard use
Debris, ash, salt water, mud and crashes can damage braking or drivetrain components. Clean/inspect before the bicycle returns to emergency reserve. Record tires, tubes, brake pads or parts consumed so the redundancy remains real.
