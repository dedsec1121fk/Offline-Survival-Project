# Inverters, DC Loads, and a Realistic Power Budget

An outage power plan fails when it counts battery capacity but ignores conversion losses, inverter standby consumption, startup surges and loads that run intermittently. Build the budget from the devices you actually intend to support.

## Measure the load path
A device powered through an inverter uses battery energy after conversion losses. A device that can safely run from a compatible DC output may avoid one conversion stage, but only use voltage, polarity and connectors intended by the manufacturer. Do not create improvised adapters for critical medical or communications equipment during an emergency.

## Separate three numbers
Record continuous watts while running, startup/surge behavior, and hours of use. Refrigerators, pumps and compressors can draw substantially more during startup than their steady state. An inverter must be compatible with both the continuous and transient demand. If the device's waveform requirements are unknown, test the exact combination before an emergency.

## Budget usable energy conservatively
Battery nameplate energy is not automatically fully usable. Chemistry, temperature, battery-management limits, age and desired depth of discharge affect what is realistically available. Use measured runtime from periodic drills to replace assumptions. Include inverter idle consumption if the inverter stays on for long periods.

## Create shedding tiers
Tier 1: medical and life-safety equipment. Tier 2: communications, minimum lighting, refrigeration when food/medicine requires it. Tier 3: information/maintenance devices. Tier 4: comfort/convenience. Define the shutdown order before the battery is low, and record the threshold that triggers each cut.
