# Smoke Inhalation and Fire Exposure: Airway Risk After Escape

Smoke can injure the airway and lungs even when there is little or no visible burn. Once outside a fire, do not let the absence of dramatic skin injury end the assessment. The priorities are clean air, rapid recognition of breathing problems, emergency evaluation when indicated, and avoiding re-entry.

## Get to clean air without re-entering
Move away from smoke and combustion products to a safe outdoor area or another location confirmed safe by responders. Do not go back inside for possessions. If the incident involved an enclosed space, synthetic materials, a generator, vehicle exhaust, or other combustion source, remember that carbon monoxide may be part of the exposure even when the smoke has cleared.

## Watch for airway and breathing deterioration
Concerning findings include difficulty breathing, persistent coughing, wheezing, hoarse voice, noisy breathing, soot around the mouth/nose, facial burns, confusion, faintness, chest pain, severe headache, or unusual sleepiness. Symptoms can worsen after the person initially appears stable. Significant enclosed-space smoke exposure, loss of consciousness, neurologic symptoms, or breathing difficulty needs urgent medical evaluation.

## First-aid boundaries
Keep the person at rest in clean air and loosen restrictive clothing. If they become unresponsive and are not breathing normally, follow CPR/AED procedures. Do not give unprescribed inhaled treatments, oxygen from an improvised source, or steam as a substitute for medical care. Cool external thermal burns with appropriate cool running water according to burn guidance, but do not delay airway assessment.

## Document the exposure
Record how long the person was in smoke if known, whether the space was enclosed, what was burning, whether there was loss of consciousness, symptoms over time, and any known asthma/cardiac/respiratory history. This history is useful when the scene is chaotic and responders see the patient later.
