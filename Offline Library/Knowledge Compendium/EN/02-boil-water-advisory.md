# Boil-water advisory: household decision flow

## Understand the advisory before acting
A boil-water advisory normally addresses microbiological risk, not every type of contamination. Read the issuing authority’s wording carefully: a “boil water” instruction is different from a “do not drink” or “do not use” notice. If chemical contamination is suspected, heating the water may not solve the problem and can concentrate some contaminants. Record the time the notice began, the affected area, and which household taps or stored containers were exposed after the notice started.

## Boiling correctly
Current CDC guidance says to bring clear water to a full rolling boil for one minute; at elevations above about 6,500 feet (2,000 m), boil for three minutes. If the water is cloudy, let solids settle or filter it through a clean cloth, paper towel, or coffee filter before boiling. Let treated water cool in a protected place and transfer it into clean, sanitized containers with tight covers. Prevent burns by keeping hot treatment vessels away from children and unstable work surfaces.

## Which household tasks need safe water
During a microbiological advisory, safe water may be needed not only for drinking but also for brushing teeth, preparing food and drinks, making ice, mixing infant formula, and washing items that contact food, depending on the official notice. Dishwashers, filters, refrigerator dispensers, ice makers, coffee machines, and plumbed appliances can retain questionable water. Follow the local authority’s instructions for flushing or cleaning those systems after the advisory ends.

## Do not let a filter create false confidence
A common household pitcher or refrigerator filter is not a substitute for boiling unless it is specifically designed and certified for the organism or hazard involved. CDC notes that boiling remains necessary during a boil-water advisory even after ordinary home filtration. A treatment chain should therefore be written as a sequence—settle/filter for clarity if needed, then disinfect by the verified method—rather than treating “filtered” as equivalent to “safe.”

## Ending the advisory
Do not stop precautions simply because the water looks normal. Wait for the issuing authority to lift the notice. When it ends, follow its instructions on flushing taps, cleaning ice makers, replacing filters, or draining appliances. Update the Water Batch log so containers made during the advisory remain identifiable and are not mistaken for water prepared after clearance.
