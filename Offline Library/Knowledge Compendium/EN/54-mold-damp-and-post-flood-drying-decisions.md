# Mold, damp materials and post-flood drying decisions

After water intrusion, the visible water may disappear while damp building materials continue to support microbial growth and structural deterioration. Drying is therefore a controlled recovery task, not simply opening every room and turning on any available fan.

## Make the area safe before drying

Confirm that electricity, structure, sewage and chemical hazards are addressed. Do not energize wet equipment or extension leads. Identify where the water came from: clean rainwater is different from sewage, floodwater or an unknown industrial source, and that difference affects what can be salvaged.

## Separate clean and dirty airflow

Moving air can help drying in appropriate conditions, but it can also spread dust or contamination from a dirty room into a clean sleeping or food area. Keep contaminated materials isolated and follow professional guidance when water was polluted. People with respiratory disease, significant allergies or immune compromise may need to avoid cleanup exposure.

## Decide what to keep

Hard, cleanable surfaces and some materials may be recoverable; deeply contaminated porous items can be much harder to restore. Do not keep an item solely because it looks dry on the surface. Record odor, visible growth, softness, swelling, delamination and moisture history. When uncertain about structural materials or extensive mold, obtain qualified assessment.

## Prevent recurrence

Find the water source before rebuilding. Photograph hidden damage as areas are safely opened, keep invoices and drying records, and avoid sealing a wall or floor while moisture remains trapped. Re-check after rain or plumbing use.

**Source anchors:** CDC environmental-health/disaster guidance and local building/public-health advice. This chapter avoids species identification and does not treat home mold tests as a substitute for correcting moisture and contamination.
