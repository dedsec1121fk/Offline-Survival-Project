# Neighborhood resource mapping: know capabilities before asking for help

## Map assets, not just hazards
A preparedness map should include safe meeting areas, water distribution points, pharmacies/clinics, public shelters, accessible routes and official notice locations where known. It can also record voluntary household capabilities such as a first-aid trained neighbor, a vehicle with extra seats or a person able to translate—only with consent.

## Protect privacy
Do not create a public list labeling a home as containing expensive equipment, medications, cash or a vulnerable person. Separate sensitive welfare information from a general resource map. Share only what is necessary with people who need it.

## Verify resources periodically
A business may close, a clinic may change hours, a road may be under construction and a neighbor may move. Date the map and recheck important points. During an incident, mark confirmed-open versus assumed-open rather than treating old information as current.

## Include constraints
A resource is useful only when its limitations are known. A well may not be potable, a generator may be reserved for one medical device, a four-wheel-drive vehicle may still be unable to cross a closed road, and a community building may not accept pets. Record constraints alongside capability.

## Build a lightweight welfare network
Agree on who checks which nearby households after a local outage, especially where older adults or people with access needs have opted in. A check should be simple and nonintrusive: status, immediate need, next contact time. Emergency services remain the path for urgent danger.

## Update after every event
Add blocked routes, useful contacts and resource failures from the incident log. Remove resources that were unreliable. A neighborhood map becomes valuable when it reflects tested reality rather than optimistic assumptions.
