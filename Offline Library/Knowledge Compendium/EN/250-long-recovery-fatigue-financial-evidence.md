# Long Recovery: Fatigue, Financial Evidence, and Decision Discipline

Weeks after the immediate hazard, recovery can become a second crisis. People are tired, temporary arrangements harden into unsafe routines, receipts disappear, repair decisions are rushed, and the same household member carries every difficult task. A recovery system should protect judgment as carefully as emergency supplies.

## Maintain a short operational rhythm
Once daily, review five things: people/health, safe housing, utilities, money/claims, and the next three priority actions. Close completed tasks and deliberately defer low-value ones. A long uncontrolled list creates the feeling that everything is urgent and hides what actually blocks recovery.

## Preserve evidence before cleanup erases it
When safe and lawful, photograph damage before disposal or repair, keep receipts/invoices, record dates and contractor/authority contacts, and link each expense to a purpose. Keep originals or protected copies of insurance, ownership/tenancy, identification and official incident documents. Do not risk entry into an unsafe structure just to improve documentation.

## Verify repair decisions
Major electrical, gas, structural, mold, sewage, roofing or other specialist work needs competent assessment. Record who inspected, what was authorized, what remains isolated, and what evidence supports reactivation. Recovery pressure can make unsafe “temporary” repairs persist for months.

## Manage financial exposure
Track committed and paid amounts separately, by currency. Verify requests for deposits or urgent payment using an independent contact channel when possible. Disaster periods attract mistakes and fraud because normal verification is harder. Avoid publishing claim numbers, identity documents or banking details in shared coordination logs.

## Rotate responsibility
Build sleep, meals, medicine, child/elder care and genuine off-duty time into the recovery plan. A person making insurance, contractor, medical and relocation decisions while chronically sleep-deprived is an operational risk. Use written handovers so another trusted person can continue without starting over.

## Define “recovered enough” milestones
Examples include safe sleeping space, verified potable water, stable medicine access, safe electrical service, a reliable route, restored documentation and a sustainable budget. Recovery is not merely returning objects to their old places; it is restoring safe, maintainable capability.
