# Blackout movement and emergency lighting: prevent injuries before restoring convenience

## Treat darkness as an immediate hazard
A sudden outage changes a familiar room into an obstacle field. Stop movement for a moment, account for people, and determine whether there is smoke, fire, structural damage, flooding or another reason to evacuate. If the building is otherwise safe, bring light to people before sending people to search for light. Children, older adults, anyone with impaired vision or balance, and anyone using mobility equipment should not be expected to navigate an unlit route alone.

## Build lighting in layers
Keep a small light where people sleep, a second light near the main exit, and a third independent light with the emergency supplies. Headlamps are useful when both hands are needed; area lanterns are better for a shared room; a low-output light can preserve batteries during a long outage. Avoid making one rechargeable device the only source. Record battery type and keep compatible spares or a charging method that does not depend on the failed mains supply.

## Protect the route, not every room
Prioritize the path between sleeping areas, toilet, water, exits, medical equipment and the main gathering point. Remove trip hazards, close unused doors, keep stairs clear and mark level changes. A bright light pointed into someone’s eyes can make adaptation worse, so illuminate walking surfaces rather than faces. Do not run extension cables across the emergency route merely to obtain more light.

## Open flame is not a normal lighting plan
Candles and improvised flames add a fire hazard exactly when alarms, water supply or response may be impaired. Prefer battery lighting. If an open flame is unavoidable for a separate essential task, it requires continuous adult supervision, a stable noncombustible location and a clear extinguishing method; it should never be left while people sleep.

## Preserve night vision and battery endurance
Use the lowest useful output. Assign one light to the active work area instead of turning on every device. Keep one fully charged light reserved for evacuation or medical tasks. If the outage may last through the night, log which lights are charged, which cells are partly used and which charging sources remain available.

## Restore safely
When power returns, do not assume every dark area is normal. Check for water near electrical equipment, damaged fixtures, unusual heat, smoke or repeated tripping. Rebuild the lighting reserve after the incident: recharge, replace depleted cells, return lights to their assigned locations and record any route that proved difficult in darkness.
