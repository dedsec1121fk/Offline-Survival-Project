# Refrigerator and freezer temperature log: replace guesswork with evidence

## Prepare before the outage
Keep an appliance thermometer in the refrigerator and freezer where it can be read quickly when power returns. Know which foods are perishable and which can tolerate room temperature. Group frozen food so the mass stays cold longer, leave unnecessary door opening for later, and record the time power fails.

## During the outage, the closed door is a control measure
USDA guidance states that an unopened refrigerator keeps food cold for about four hours, while a full freezer can hold temperature for roughly 48 hours and a half-full freezer for about 24 hours. These are planning figures, not a guarantee for every appliance or ambient condition. A thermometer and actual food condition are stronger evidence than memory.

## Build a simple log
Record outage start, any known door openings, appliance temperature when observed without unnecessary opening, power-restoration time and the temperature when power returns. For the freezer, note whether packages still contain ice crystals when assessed. Do not repeatedly open the door “to check”; that consumes the cold reserve you are trying to measure.

## Separate safe decisions from salvage pressure
When the evidence shows that a perishable food has been too warm for too long, discard it according to current food-safety guidance. Appearance, smell or a taste test cannot reliably prove safety. Do not prioritize the purchase price over foodborne-illness risk, especially for children, older adults, pregnant people or anyone with reduced immunity.

## A cooler is another cold chain, not magic storage
If moving food to a cooler, record when it moved, the cooling source and temperature if available. Prevent meltwater from contaminating food packaging. Replenishing ice resets neither an unknown earlier exposure nor the history of a food that was already warm.

## After power returns
Stabilize the appliances, clean spills, document discarded high-value items if needed for recovery records, and restock only after the unit returns to a safe operating range. Review whether more thermal mass, thermometers or a better “use first” plan would have reduced loss.
