# Roof and Ladder Decisions After Storm, Fire, or Earthquake Damage

Post-disaster property inspection creates a strong temptation to climb for a better view. Damaged roofs, wet tiles, loose parapets, hidden structural weakness, nearby power lines, smoke damage, and wind can turn a routine ladder task into a high-consequence fall. The safest inspection is often from the ground with photographs or by a qualified professional.

## Set a no-climb threshold
Do not climb when the roof or supporting structure may be fire-, earthquake-, or water-damaged; when surfaces are wet/icy; when wind is strong; when power lines are nearby; when the ladder cannot stand on firm level ground; or when the person is fatigued, injured, or working alone. A damaged building is not the place to learn ladder technique for the first time.

## Use remote observation first
From a safe position, document displaced roofing, sagging, cracks, fallen masonry, damaged solar panels/antennas, water entry, and debris. Mark interior areas below suspected damage as restricted until assessed. If tarping or emergency repair requires roof access, treat it as skilled work and use professionals where available rather than trading property protection for a serious injury.

## Keep the ground zone controlled
Falling tiles, glass, branches, tools, and sections of gutter can injure people below even when no one is on the roof. Establish a perimeter and keep children, animals, and unnecessary helpers away. Watch for overhead electrical service and damaged trees before moving long ladders or poles.

## Verification anchors
- CDC/NIOSH disaster cleanup guidance emphasizes hazard inspection and avoiding unsafe fire-damaged roofs/structures.
- Qualified structural, roofing, electrical, and fire professionals should control return-to-work decisions after significant damage.
