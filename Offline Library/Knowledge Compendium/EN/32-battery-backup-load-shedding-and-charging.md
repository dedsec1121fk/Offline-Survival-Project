# Battery backup: energy budgeting, load shedding, and safe charging

## Start with watt-hours, not wishful runtime
A battery system is finite stored energy. List each load’s approximate watts and expected hours of use, then calculate watt-hours. Keep a reserve rather than planning to discharge every available unit. Inverters and conversion losses mean usable energy can be lower than the label suggests, and battery capacity can change with temperature, age and discharge rate.

## Create power tiers
Tier 1 should contain functions whose loss changes safety or medical continuity: prescribed medical devices, essential communications, emergency lighting or temperature-sensitive medicines when applicable. Tier 2 can cover refrigeration cycles, limited networking and work needed for household continuity. Tier 3 contains comfort and discretionary loads. When available energy falls, disable entire lower tiers instead of negotiating every device repeatedly.

## Charge deliberately
Use chargers, cables and power supplies intended for the battery chemistry and product. Keep charging equipment dry, ventilated as required by the manufacturer and away from flammable clutter. Stop using visibly swollen, damaged, leaking or abnormally hot battery packs. Do not improvise cell-level repairs to lithium packs during an emergency.

## Measure the real system
Record state of charge at fixed times, energy source, major loads and charging windows. If the system loses 20 percent overnight when the plan predicted 8 percent, change the plan immediately: locate hidden loads, reduce runtime or find more charging capacity. The Command Center’s Power Operations section can compare stored watt-hours with daily load demand and reserve.

## Solar and vehicle charging are conditional resources
Solar output depends on weather, panel orientation, shading, temperature and controller limits. Vehicle charging consumes vehicle fuel or traction energy and may affect evacuation range. Treat both as inputs with opportunity costs, not free unlimited power.
