# Flood Re-entry: Moisture, Mold, Structure, and Work-Zone Control

Re-entering a flooded building is a staged safety decision, not the start of cleanup by default. Floodwater can combine structural damage, energized electrical equipment, sewage, sharp debris, unstable floors, mold, fuel residues, and poor indoor air. The first question is whether the building is safe to enter at all.

## Separate inspection from cleanup
Before moving belongings, identify structural, electrical, gas, and contamination hazards. Do not enter standing water to reach an electrical panel. If the building was closed for days, CDC advises airing it before prolonged occupancy and treating mold as likely after significant wetting. Photograph major damage before disposal when this can be done safely, especially if insurance or assistance documentation will be required.

## Stop moisture before cosmetic repair
Drying and removal of water-damaged material are more important than hiding stains or spraying fragrance. Wet porous materials can hold contamination and moisture behind visible surfaces. Create “clean,” “dirty,” and disposal paths so contaminated debris does not pass through sleeping/food areas. People with asthma, severe allergies, immune compromise, or other relevant conditions may need to avoid mold cleanup and seek professional advice.

## Electrical restoration is a separate gate
A dry-looking outlet or appliance is not proof of electrical safety. Flooded circuits and equipment require qualified inspection before re-energization. Keep generators outdoors and away from openings; cleanup often creates CO incidents when power is unavailable and fuel-powered equipment is brought too close to a building.

## Close the recovery loop
Maintain a moisture/drying log, photos, discarded-item list, professional inspection notes, and the date each zone was returned to service. If odor, visible growth, recurrent dampness, electrical problems, or structural movement persists, the zone remains unresolved rather than being marked “clean.”
