# Fatigue, sleep and shift-handover control

Long emergencies are often damaged by ordinary fatigue rather than dramatic equipment failure. Tired people miss medication times, repeat work, forget a shutoff, misread messages and take risks they would reject when rested. A household therefore needs a rest plan as much as a task list.

## Protect minimum functioning

Identify tasks that truly require continuous watch: fire/smoke risk, a medically vulnerable person, rapidly changing weather or security. Everything else should be grouped into scheduled checks so at least one capable person can sleep. Do not make the most exhausted person the permanent night watch simply because they volunteered first.

## Make shifts explicit

Write who is responsible, start/end time, what must be checked, what conditions wake the next person and what equipment changes hands. Keep the shift short enough that attention remains useful. If only one adult is available, simplify the operation: reduce optional tasks, use alarms where safe and seek outside support rather than pretending continuous vigilance is possible indefinitely.

## Handover before rest

The outgoing person states people accounted for, urgent hazards, medical issues, water/food/power status, active decisions, next official-information check and any unresolved problem. The incoming person repeats the critical points back. This prevents a notebook entry from being mistaken for actual understanding.

## Watch for degraded judgement

Repeated mistakes, irritability, microsleeps, confusion, unsafe shortcuts and inability to remember the last action are operational warnings. Stop complex work and reassign or rest when possible. Severe confusion or unusual medical symptoms require appropriate medical evaluation rather than being labelled “just tired.”

**Source anchors:** emergency-management human-factors practice, WHO psychosocial/emergency principles and local occupational-safety guidance.
