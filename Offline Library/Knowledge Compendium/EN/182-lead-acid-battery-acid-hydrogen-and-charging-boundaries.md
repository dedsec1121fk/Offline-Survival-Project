# Lead-Acid Batteries: Acid, Hydrogen, and Charging Boundaries

Lead-acid batteries remain common in cars, UPS systems and some solar installations. Their hazards differ from lithium systems: corrosive electrolyte, very high short-circuit current, heavy weight and hydrogen that can be produced during charging.

## Before touching the system
Identify whether the battery is flooded, AGM, gel or another type and follow the manufacturer's charging limits. Remove jewelry that could bridge terminals. Keep metal tools controlled. Eye protection and appropriate gloves matter when working around exposed electrolyte. Never improvise an unknown charger profile because “12 volts is 12 volts.”

## Ventilation is part of the system
Do not charge a vented lead-acid battery in a sealed living compartment. Keep sparks, cigarettes and flame away from a charging bank. A power cabinet that traps gases is not made safe by simply closing its door. If a system was flooded, physically damaged or has a cracked case, isolate it from routine use until qualified assessment.

## Acid exposure
Battery electrolyte is corrosive. If exposure occurs, begin immediate first-aid flushing appropriate to the body area and obtain professional/poison guidance. Do not neutralize chemicals on skin or eyes with improvised mixtures. Contaminated clothing should be handled so acid is not transferred to another person.

## Field continuity practice
Label each bank with nominal voltage, chemistry, charger, disconnect point and last inspection. Track resting voltage only if you understand the system and know that voltage alone does not prove capacity. Plan lifting/movement for the actual battery mass; dropped batteries create both electrical and chemical hazards.
