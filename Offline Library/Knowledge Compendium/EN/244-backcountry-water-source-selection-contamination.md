# Backcountry Water: Source Selection and Contamination Boundaries

Clear mountain water can contain disease-causing organisms, while chemically contaminated water may not be made safe by ordinary filters or boiling. Field water planning begins with choosing the least questionable source available and understanding what the chosen treatment can and cannot remove.

## Prefer a better source before using more treatment
When possible choose flowing water away from obvious human/animal waste, camps, mines, industrial sites, agricultural runoff, flood contamination and stagnant scum. Collect upstream from your own washing/toilet area. A source with visible fuel sheen, chemical odor, unusual color, dead animals or known industrial contamination should not be “fixed” by routine backpacking treatment.

## Microbial treatment has limits
Boiling and approved disinfection methods address many biological hazards when performed correctly; filters have specific pore sizes and organism claims. Follow the device or public-health instructions. Turbid water may need settling/prefiltration before final treatment. No household method should be assumed to remove every chemical, salt, heavy metal or toxin.

## Keep treated and untreated systems separate
Use a marked dirty collection container/bag and a clean treated-water container. Do not let dirty hose ends, bottle threads or hands re-contaminate the clean side. Record treatment time and batch when multiple people share the system.

## Carry reserve for treatment failure
Filters clog, batteries die, tablets expire and fuel runs out. A route that depends on one treatment method and one water source has a single point of failure. Carry a compatible backup method appropriate to the environment.

## Illness after field water
Persistent vomiting/diarrhea, blood in stool, fever, severe abdominal pain, dehydration or illness in a vulnerable person requires medical advice. Record which source and treatment were used so clinicians/public-health workers have useful exposure information.
