# Circulatory Shock: Recognition and Support While Help Is Coming

Shock is a life-threatening failure of circulation that can follow severe bleeding, major trauma, burns, severe allergic reactions, infection, or other emergencies. The field task is to recognize deterioration, address the obvious cause when possible, prevent additional stress, and get emergency care.

## Recognize the pattern, not one number
Possible signs include rapid weak pulse, fast breathing, pale/gray or cool clammy skin, weakness, dizziness, thirst, anxiety, confusion, nausea, or decreasing responsiveness. Skin signs can look different across skin tones and may be less obvious in heat. Do not wait for every sign to appear.

## Treat the cause you can safely treat
Control severe external bleeding with direct pressure and appropriate bleeding-control methods. For anaphylaxis, follow the person's prescribed epinephrine emergency plan. For burns or trauma, stop ongoing exposure and avoid unnecessary movement. These actions do not replace emergency medical care; they reduce continuing injury while help is activated.

## Position and protect
If the person is breathing normally and has no injury or breathing problem that makes the position unsafe, keep them lying down and comfortable. Do not force leg elevation when trauma, pain, breathing difficulty, pregnancy, or other circumstances make it inappropriate. Protect from cold ground and wind, but avoid overheating.

## Nothing by mouth during serious deterioration
Do not give food or drink to a person with suspected shock, major trauma, reduced consciousness, or likely urgent procedure. Monitor breathing and responsiveness continuously. If they become unresponsive and are not breathing normally, begin CPR and use an AED if available.

## Handoff record
Record the suspected cause, bleeding amount if estimable, treatments performed, changes in responsiveness, breathing and pulse quality, allergies/medicines if known, and times of major events. A short timeline is often more useful than a long narrative.
