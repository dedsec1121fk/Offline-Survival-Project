# Concussion Observation and Handoff After a Head Impact

A concussion can occur without a dramatic loss of consciousness. After a head impact, the practical offline task is to recognize deterioration, prevent another injury, document what changed, and arrange appropriate medical evaluation rather than trying to “test” the person back into activity.

## Establish a baseline immediately
Record the time and mechanism of the hit. Ask simple orientation questions and note the person's normal behavior, speech, balance, memory, headache, vision, nausea and responsiveness. If witnesses saw a loss of consciousness, seizure, unusual movement, or repeated impact, record that separately instead of relying on the injured person's memory.

## Red flags require urgent escalation
Emergency evaluation is especially important for worsening or severe headache, repeated vomiting, seizure, increasing confusion/agitation, inability to stay awake, weakness/numbness, slurred speech, unequal pupils, worsening coordination, clear fluid/blood from ears or nose after significant trauma, or any rapid deterioration. Anticoagulant use, older age, or a high-energy mechanism lowers the threshold for professional assessment.

## Protect the brain from another hit
Stop sports, climbing, cycling, driving, machinery, or other activities where impaired reaction or a second impact could cause more harm. Do not use alcohol or sedating recreational drugs to “help sleep.” Follow professional advice for rest and gradual return to normal activity; outdated rules that everyone must be kept awake all night are not a substitute for medical assessment.

## Observation needs an accountable person
A responsible adult should know what symptoms to watch, when the last check occurred, and what would trigger emergency help. During a wider disaster, write the observations into the incident log so a shift change does not erase the history.

## Handoff packet
Provide the injury time, mechanism, witnessed loss of consciousness, vomiting/seizures, symptom trend, medications, previous concussion history if known, and any new neurologic change. Do not let a normal-looking photograph or a brief period of feeling better erase the documented trend.
