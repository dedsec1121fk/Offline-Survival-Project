# Solar Charging in the Field: Setup, Yield, and Connector Discipline

A portable solar panel is not a guaranteed number of watt-hours. Output changes with sun angle, cloud, shade, panel temperature, charge-controller limits, battery state and conversion losses. Survival planning should therefore use measured performance from your own equipment, not only the number printed on the panel.

## Build a measured baseline
On a normal clear day, record panel rating, controller, battery, time window, weather and actual energy delivered if your equipment can report it. Repeat in imperfect conditions. The useful result is a range: what your system normally produces, what it produces under poor conditions and which loads must be cut when generation falls below demand.

## Shade matters disproportionately
Partial shade across a panel can reduce output far more than the shaded area might suggest, depending on panel design. Inspect the site repeatedly as the sun moves. Keep panels where they will not become a trip hazard, vehicle hazard or windborne object. Secure portable panels against gusts without puncturing or bending them.

## Respect electrical compatibility
Confirm panel open-circuit voltage/current and controller input limits before connecting unfamiliar panels in series or parallel. Do not improvise polarity. Do not mate visually similar connectors unless their pinout and electrical ratings are known. Stop using cables that become unusually hot or have damaged insulation.

## Operational priority
Charge the storage system first when doing so gives you better control over critical loads; direct charging may be appropriate for equipment designed for it. Reserve energy for communications, medical equipment, lighting and information before comfort loads. Recalculate the plan after two poor solar days rather than waiting for the battery to reach an emergency threshold.
