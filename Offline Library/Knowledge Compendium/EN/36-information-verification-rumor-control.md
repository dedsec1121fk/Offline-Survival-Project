# Information verification during disasters: separating signal from rumor

## Treat information as an operational resource
Bad information can cause unnecessary evacuation, unsafe travel, panic buying or delayed protective action. Record each important claim with a source and timestamp instead of allowing it to become “everyone says.” A screenshot without origin or time is not the same as a current official instruction.

## Source hierarchy
For immediate protective actions, prefer the authority responsible for that hazard and location: civil protection/emergency service, fire service, police, water utility, electricity operator, municipality, meteorological service or public-health agency. News reporting can provide context, but it should not override a newer official evacuation order or boil-water notice.

## Verify before amplifying
Before forwarding a claim, ask: who issued it, when, for which geographic area, and is there a second reliable confirmation? Old images and messages frequently reappear during new incidents. Check dates, landmarks and whether the message names a zone that actually includes your location.

## Maintain an incident information log
Write only the facts that change decisions: “112 alert 18:42—evacuate Zone A toward north,” “utility notice 19:05—water not for drinking,” “municipality 20:10—shelter full.” Keep speculation in a separate note if it must be recorded. The next household shift should be able to see why a decision was made without reconstructing social-media history.

## When networks fail
Preselect local radio frequencies, physical notice locations, municipal meeting points and trusted neighbours. If a message comes through a messenger, ask the sender whether they saw the original source or are repeating someone else. Offline resilience includes a way to update knowledge without the internet, not merely storing old web pages.
