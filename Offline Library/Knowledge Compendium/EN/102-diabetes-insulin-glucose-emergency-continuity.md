# Diabetes, insulin, and glucose-management continuity during emergencies

## Treat continuity as a clinical dependency, not a generic food problem
Power loss, evacuation, heat, disrupted meals, stress, damaged pharmacies, and reduced access to clean water can all change diabetes management at once. Before an incident, keep an up-to-date medication/device list, clinician and pharmacy contacts, normal supplies, prescription information, and a written sick-day or emergency plan supplied by the person’s clinical team. The household plan should preserve access to care; it must not invent dose changes.

## Insulin storage needs a documented contingency
CDC advises keeping insulin as cool as practical during a power outage while avoiding freezing, direct heat, and direct sunlight. Different insulin products and delivery systems can have different storage limits, so the product label, pharmacist, manufacturer, and treating clinician remain the authority for a specific product. A cooler is not automatically safe if it can freeze the medicine. Record any period when storage conditions were uncertain and replace affected supplies as professional guidance recommends.

## Keep the entire treatment chain together
A diabetes kit may involve insulin or other medicines, glucose meter/continuous-monitor supplies, test strips, lancets, charging cables, batteries, pump/infusion supplies, glucagon if prescribed, identification, snacks or glucose sources specified by the care plan, and sharps disposal. Store compatible spares together and note which components expire. A working phone does not help if the only proprietary charger or sensor is left behind.

## Food and hydration changes require earlier monitoring, not improvisation
Emergency meals may contain more carbohydrate, less variety, or irregular timing. Heat illness, infection, vomiting, dehydration, and unusual physical exertion can further change glucose control. Follow the individual clinical plan for monitoring and sick days. Do not use an offline manual to decide insulin corrections, alter basal settings, or substitute medicines.

## Identify escalation triggers before transport is difficult
The person and caregiver should know the clinician-defined signs that require urgent advice or emergency care, especially persistent extreme glucose readings, inability to keep fluids down, altered consciousness, severe weakness, breathing difficulty, suspected ketoacidosis, or other serious symptoms. If transport routes may close, the evacuation threshold should be earlier than the point at which the last supply or battery is exhausted.

## Recovery includes replacing uncertain stock and restoring routine
After the incident, reconcile medicines and devices, replace anything damaged by heat/water, restore normal refill timing, download or preserve relevant monitoring data if useful to the clinical team, and document the infrastructure failure that created the highest risk.
