# Choking response: recognition, training, and handover

## Distinguish effective coughing from severe obstruction
A person who can cough forcefully or speak still has some airflow and should generally be encouraged to keep coughing while being closely observed. Severe choking may present with inability to speak, cough effectively, or breathe, with silent distress and rapidly worsening colour or responsiveness. Activate emergency help when the obstruction is severe.

## Adult and child techniques should be learned hands-on
Current American Red Cross guidance for a responsive choking adult or child uses cycles of five back blows and five abdominal thrusts when appropriate. Technique changes for infants and for some body types or conditions, which is why a household reference should point toward current first-aid training rather than compress all variations into one generic instruction. Practice with training equipment, not on healthy people with force.

## Never perform a blind finger sweep
Do not reach into the mouth for an object you cannot see. Blind sweeps can push an obstruction deeper or injure tissue. If an object is clearly visible and easily removable, follow current first-aid training. If the person becomes unresponsive, the response transitions to CPR/AED procedures and emergency medical care according to training.

## Plan for children and high-risk meals
Households with young children should control food size and eating posture, keep small non-food objects out of reach, and ensure caregivers know the infant-specific response. During outages, dim lighting and hurried eating can increase risk; avoid feeding while walking, driving, or during chaotic evacuation movement.

## Document after the event
Even if the object is expelled and the person appears better, professional assessment may be needed after a severe choking event, especially if symptoms persist. Record what happened, any first-aid actions, and whether the person lost responsiveness so responders receive an accurate handover.
