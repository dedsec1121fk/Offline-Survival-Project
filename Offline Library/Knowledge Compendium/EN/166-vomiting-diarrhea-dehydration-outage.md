# Vomiting, Diarrhea, and Dehydration During an Outage

Gastrointestinal illness becomes more dangerous when safe water, refrigeration, toilets, pharmacies, and transport are disrupted. The operational goals are to prevent contamination from spreading, maintain safe hydration when possible, and recognize when home management is no longer appropriate.

## Stop the contamination chain
Assign a separate toilet if available; otherwise clean high-touch bathroom surfaces with products used exactly according to their labels and never mix cleaning chemicals. Wash hands with soap and safe water, especially after toileting/diapering and before food preparation. A symptomatic food handler should not prepare meals for the group. Keep vomit/diarrhea cleanup materials away from food and drinking-water zones.

## Protect the water source
Use water that authorities consider safe. If a boil-water or do-not-use advisory is active, follow that specific advisory rather than a generic treatment rule. Do not make infant formula, ice, oral medicines, or drinks with water known or suspected to be unsafe. When commercial oral rehydration solution is available, use it according to its label/clinical advice; do not improvise concentrated salt/sugar mixtures by guesswork.

## Watch for dangerous dehydration
Urgent evaluation is warranted when the person cannot keep fluids down, becomes very weak, confused, unusually sleepy, has signs of shock, severe or persistent abdominal pain, blood in stool/vomit, or markedly reduced urination. Infants, young children, older adults, pregnant people, and people with kidney, heart, or metabolic disease can deteriorate faster and need a lower threshold for professional advice.

## Log the cluster
Record who became ill, approximate onset, foods/water shared, fever if measured, and whether multiple people are affected. A cluster may indicate contaminated food/water rather than an isolated stomach illness. Stop serving the suspected source and contact public-health/medical services when available.
